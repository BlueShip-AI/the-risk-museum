Working Paper No. 32 · 14 August 2026 · two registered attempts to trade the tail

The Tail Wags at the Close

Leveraged funds must trade with every day’s move, and Morgan Stanley says they sold $100 billion of equities in eight weeks. I tested whether daily prices can harvest the reversal, twice, under a bar that rose with the second attempt. One test died of costs to the basis point. One lived inside a single regime. The mechanism stands, unrefuted, in the minutes daily bars cannot see.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

Abstract. Morgan Stanley’s derivatives desk published the numbers behind a growing worry: leveraged ETFs, now over $200 billion, must trade in the same direction as every day’s move, and sold over $100 billion of equities in eight weeks this summer. The tail, people say, is wagging the dog. I tested whether a daily-bar trader can be paid for knowing this, twice, under a significance bar that rose with the second attempt. Both tests failed, informatively. The first found a real gross effect that trading costs consume to the basis point. The second, using real fund assets from SEC filings, found returns that live almost entirely inside one regime: 2020 supplied 113% of its lifetime profit. My own reviewer then showed both versions collapse to a naive bet against big moves, and caught two bugs in my work along the way. The mechanism is real and unrefuted. It plays out in the minutes around the close, where daily prices cannot reach, and Wall Street has begun paying billions to own the firms that manufacture such flows.

1. The dog and the tail

A fund promising L times a day’s index move must rebalance at every close, buying after up days and selling after down days, in an amount equal to (L squared minus L) times its assets times the day’s return. That is arithmetic, not strategy, and it holds for leveraged and inverse funds alike, so all of them trade the same direction at once. Morgan Stanley’s quantitative desk put scale on it in August 2026: over $200 billion in these funds, over $100 billion of global equities sold from June 5 to July 29, nearly $30 billion bought on July 30 alone, and 2 to 4% of total US equity volume on peak days. Their conclusion: structural flows now matter as much as fundamentals. The question this paper can answer is narrower: can a trader holding daily prices harvest the reversal this flow should cause?

2. Two tests, one bar that rose

The first test scaled the pressure signal by a fund-volume proxy and faded it: 4,141 trading days, net minus 0.11% a year. The autopsy was the interesting part. Gross, the strategy earned 7.5% a year with stable out-of-sample performance; trading costs took 7.53%. The breakeven commission was 4.96 basis points against the 5 charged. Cost-death to the basis point, the third such verdict in this pipeline’s records.

The second test replaced the proxy with real assets, quarterly net-asset filings from the SEC for all thirteen funds, and paid a higher significance bar for being the second look at the same idea. It made money on paper, 6% a year, and failed anyway: a t-statistic of 0.89 against the required 2.74, and a sample split showing the edge exists only before late 2024. One year, 2020, contributed 113% of the strategy’s lifetime profit, and the book’s first active day was March 3, 2020, the week of the crash reversals. One regime, harvested once.

3. What the reviewer caught

This pipeline runs an independent automated reviewer whose only job is to attack results, and it earned its keep three times here. It showed the first test’s volume proxy was a volatility filter in disguise. It showed my "real assets" were partly a price series in costume, because scaling quarterly filings by fund prices discards the one thing, investor flows, that makes assets different from price. And it found that a filter using no fund data at all, just "the index moved a lot today," beat both versions on the same window. It also caught two errors of mine now on the permanent record: a wrong provenance label, and asset anchors applied before their filings were public. Since the pressure’s direction always equals the day’s move by construction, every daily-bar version of this idea is a bet against big moves behind some gate, and the reviewer’s ruling closed the family at this data frequency.

4. What stands

Two honest rejections establish that daily closing prices cannot harvest this flow at realistic costs. They do not refute the mechanism: Morgan Stanley’s flow numbers stand, and neither test ever observed an actual flow. The predicted capture window is the close auction and the following open, minutes this museum’s daily data cannot see, a boundary this site mapped in an earlier paper. Meanwhile the market itself has voted on whether structural flow matters: Goldman Sachs just agreed to pay up to $2.3 billion for NEOS, a firm founded in 2022 running $30 billion of option-income ETFs, its second such acquisition in months. Mechanical flow is now a product category. The next testable version of this idea runs through options data and roll calendars, files under the same family, and pays the higher bar that a third look costs.

5. What this does not establish

Nothing here measures the close auction itself, actual creations and redemptions, or intraday reversion; those need finer data than daily bars, and buying that data is a separate decision. The 5 basis point cost assumption is, if anything, generous to the strategy for trading into the very auction the flow crowds. And the two rejections say nothing about whether the flow moves prices, only that its echo in daily closes is too small and too era-bound to pay for the trading it requires.

Provenance, bs-prov/1.0

Method
Two registered tests of one declared idea, judged 14 August 2026 at family-deflated bars of 2.50 and 2.74. Verdicts, diagnostics, the cost-death arithmetic (gross 7.5% a year against 7.53% of costs, breakeven 4.96 basis points), the one-regime finding (2020 supplied 113% of lifetime profit), and both reviewer interventions are recorded in the pipeline’s cycle report and hypothesis database, including two errors of the author’s the reviewer caught.
Sources
Fund assets from SEC N-PORT filings, thirteen funds, quarterly, late 2019 through mid 2026. Mechanism: Cheng and Madhavan, 2009; Tuzun, Federal Reserve, 2013. Flow figures: Morgan Stanley QDS, August 2026. Acquisition figures: Goldman Sachs press release and Bloomberg, August 2026.
Honesty
Neither test observed an actual flow; both inferred pressure from prices and assets. The close auction, creations and redemptions, and intraday reversion need finer data than daily bars. The rejections close the daily-bar version, not the mechanism.
