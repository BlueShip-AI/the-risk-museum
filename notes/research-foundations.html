The Museum’s Method

by V. Dimopoulos · Research note #12 · a foundations note on causal inference, research design, and choosing the right test · July 25, 2026

Every other note in this wing reports a result. This one reports the method those results are held to. A backtest is a correlation with good production values. The job of a research process is to decide which correlations deserve belief, and that decision rests on three older disciplines than quantitative finance. Causal inference tells you what a result would have to be to mean anything. Research design tells you to settle the question before you touch the data. Test selection tells you which statistic answers the question you actually asked. The museum’s gates are those three ideas wearing a trading costume.

Start from the counterfactual

Murnane and Willett open Methods Matter with a single demand. Before you claim that something caused something, state the counterfactual, what would have happened to the same subject under the other condition. You never observe it, so the whole craft of causal work is building a credible stand-in for it. Their toolkit runs from the randomized experiment, which manufactures the counterfactual by coin flip, down through natural experiments, the regression-discontinuity design, difference-in-differences, and instrumental-variables estimation, each a way to find variation that is “arguably exogenous” when you cannot randomize.

Markets rarely hand you a clean experiment. A signal that worked is entangled with the regime that produced it, and the confounds are the whole game. So the museum leans on the weaker instruments honestly. Out-of-sample periods, regime splits, and a stressed rerun with the edge halved and costs doubled are our imperfect counterfactuals, and we label them as imperfect. The strongest of them is the one Campbell Harvey insists on, an economic reason the edge should exist before the test is run. A pattern with no mechanism is a confound you have not found yet.

Design before data

Creswell’s Research Design is a book about committing to the plan first. Name the question, state the hypothesis, choose the approach, and write down what would count as failure, all before the evidence can talk you into a story. Quantitative confirmatory work like ours lives at the strict end of his spectrum, and it inherits his checklist of validity threats. Internal validity asks whether the effect is real or an artifact of the sample. External validity asks whether it travels beyond the window you fit. Construct validity asks whether the variable measures what you named it. Statistical-conclusion validity asks whether the test was even appropriate. Our pre-registration files are that checklist, written before a single filing is downloaded, which is why a study can come back significant and still be shelved.

Choose the test that fits the question

Once the design is fixed, the statistic is close to mechanical. The choice falls out of two questions, what kind of outcome you are measuring and how many groups or what kind of predictor you are comparing. The table below is the working version of the decision charts every applied statistician keeps taped to a wall.

Categoricalvs a categorical predictor, two independent groupsChi-squared (Fisher’s exact for small cells)McNemar’s for paired data

The parametric column assumes independent observations, rough normality, and comparable variances. When those fail, you transform the data or move one column right to the rank-based test. Reading a strategy’s daily returns is the bottom row, a continuous outcome against continuous predictors, so the museum lives in regression with one repair the textbook chart does not draw. Financial returns are autocorrelated and heteroskedastic, so the ordinary t-statistic overstates significance. Every gate here uses a Newey-West correction and a block bootstrap in its place, which is the honest version of that same bottom row.

Then raise the bar for how many tests you ran

The chart picks the test. It says nothing about how many tests you ran to get there, and that omission is where most published edges die. Harvey, Liu and Zhu showed that once you count the hundreds of factors the field has tried, the ordinary threshold of about two standard errors is far too generous, and a real discovery needs closer to three. The museum goes further and asks a survivor to clear roughly a t of five after the stressed rerun, and it records the size of the family it searched so the deflation is honest. A p-value earned in isolation and a p-value earned after a thousand attempts are not the same number, even when the arithmetic agrees.

Tools worth keeping on the desk

The frameworks above are cheap to apply once you have the right instruments. Four that hold up.

  • Laerd Statistics walks you from a description of your data to the correct test, then shows the software steps to run it. The test selector is the interactive form of the table above.
  • Julius AI turns a dataset into charts and forecasts quickly and will confirm and run a test for you, useful for a first pass before you formalize it.
  • Oslo Metropolitan University’s open methods modules are short and practical, a fast refresher on when each test applies.
  • Khan Academy statistics and probability covers the ground under all of it, from summarizing data to inference.
Why this hangs in the museum. The other notes earn trust by showing their numbers. This one earns it by showing the rules those numbers were scored against, because a result is only as credible as the method that would have rejected it. Everything in this wing was designed to be able to fail, and most of it did.
