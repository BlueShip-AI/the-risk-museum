Measuring the Short Side: Daily Consolidated Short-Volume
Crowding as a Risk Measure, Not a Tradable Signal

I tested four ways to trade public short selling data. None worked. It measures risk, not profit.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

Abstract. I test whether daily consolidated short volume, published without charge by the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) under Regulation SHO, carries cross-sectional information, information about which stocks will do better than which others, for US large-cap stocks. On a panel of 2,006 trading days and 406 stocks (2018–2026), I define a short volume ratio (SVR) as short volume divided by total volume and, following the literature on informed short sellers (Boehmer, Huszar and Jordan, 2010), fix the direction in advance: long (buy) where SVR is low, short (bet on a decline) where it is high. Four smoothed transforms are declared in advance and judged against a pass/fail hurdle of Newey–West t ≥ 2.96, where t is the standard measure of whether a result is distinguishable from luck; the bar is set this high because four tests of one idea mean four chances to get lucky. Zero of the four clear it; the statistics range from −2.24 to 0.18. In the nine worst months for an equally weighted market, however, the quintile shorted most heavily (stocks are sorted into five equal buckets by the measure) outperforms the one shorted least by 220 basis points (hundredths of one percent) per month (t = 5.34), the signature of crowded short sellers buying back shares to close their positions (covering) under stress. Because the universe is current index constituents (heavily shorted stocks that survived their squeezes rather than those that collapsed and delisted), that estimate is biased upward. I classify crowding on the short side as a risk measure, not a signal.

Keywords: short volume, Regulation SHO, FINRA, crowding on the short side, short squeeze, multiple testing, survivorship bias.

1. Introduction

Short sellers are, on average, informed. Heavily shorted stocks subsequently underperform (Boehmer, Huszar and Jordan, 2010), and the daily flow of short sales predicts returns at short horizons (Diether, Lee and Werner, 2009). Those results rest on monthly short interest or on proprietary order flow. FINRA publishes a free daily alternative, consolidated short volume from the Regulation SHO tapes. This paper asks the narrow question whether that public proxy, measured daily, reproduces the same predictability in large-cap stocks, or whether it merely reprices what is already crowded. The study is pre-registered: one measure, four transforms of it fixed in advance, the trade direction fixed in advance, and the pass/fail hurdle set before any return was computed.

2. Data

The panel is FINRA daily consolidated short volume (Regulation SHO), covering 2,006 trading days and 406 large-cap stocks from 2018 through 2026. Consolidated (CDN) tape coverage begins in 2018; this is a stated limit that confines the sample to the window from 2018 onward and excludes every earlier stress episode, so the regime evidence below rests entirely on the drawdowns within this window. For each stock and day the short volume ratio SVR is short volume divided by total reported volume, smoothed before use.

3. Results

3.1 Cross-sectional tests

Four transforms of SVR were declared in advance: the smoothed level over 21 and 63 trading days, the change over 21 days, and a level made neutral within sector. Each is sorted in the direction fixed in advance and evaluated against a raised pass/fail hurdle of Newey–West t ≥ 2.96, the level appropriate to four tests of one idea. None clears it. The largest magnitude, the change over 21 days at t = −2.24, still falls short of the bar; the two level tests are indistinguishable from zero. Crowding in short volume does not forecast which large-cap stocks beat which others in this window.

3.2 Behaviour in stress

The signal is silent on average but not in the extremes. In the nine worst months of the sample for an equally weighted market, the quintile shorted most heavily outperforms the one shorted least by 220 basis points per month, with a Newey–West t-statistic of 5.34. The sign is the opposite of what a tradable short position would need. The most heavily shorted stocks gain precisely when the market falls, the mechanical signature of forced covering as short positions are unwound under stress.

4. Verdict and discussion

Only one statistic in the study clears the bar, and it points the wrong way for a signal. The most heavily shorted stocks rise in stress rather than falling. That is a property of the stress state itself, not an advantage a trading desk can capture, and it is contaminated by survivorship in a way set out in Section 5. The finding is therefore held, not promoted: kept on record as a fact about risk, not advanced to a tradable strategy. It mirrors this site’s crowding study built on the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) form on which large managers disclose US equity holdings each quarter (13F), where an effect that was statistically real was retained as a risk measure rather than put forward as a tradable product. A risk desk learns from it where covering pressure concentrates; a trading desk learns nothing it can act on.

5. Limitations

The binding limitation is survivorship. The universe is current index constituents, so it retains only the heavily shorted stocks that survived their squeezes; the stocks that collapsed under short pressure and delisted are absent by construction. The +220 basis point stress estimate is measured on the survivors of exactly the event it describes and is therefore an upper bound, not a neutral figure. Two further limits are structural: consolidated coverage begins only in 2018, so the sample cannot see earlier crises, and short volume is a flow proxy for positioning, not a direct measure of short interest or of economic exposure through derivatives. A third structural limit is statistical. The stress estimate rests on nine monthly observations, and the Newey-West correction at that length draws on two lags. Corrections of that kind are large-sample results, so at nine observations the reported t of 5.34 does not carry the distribution its size implies. It is reported here as a magnitude, not as evidence of significance, and nothing in the verdict rests on it.

This study is retained because a naive read of t = 5.34 looks like a green light. It is the opposite. The number is large because the sample has already discarded the shorts that were right. Measuring crowding on the survivors of the squeeze is how a risk measure gets mistaken for a signal.
