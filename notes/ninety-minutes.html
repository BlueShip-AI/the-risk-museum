Ninety Minutes Without a Number:
Permissioned Abundance and the Discipline of Writing It Down

V. Dimopoulos
BlueShip Research
Research Note No. 20 · 3 August 2026

In one line: Intelligence is getting cheap and permission is getting scarce, and I wrote fourteen dated, scoreable claims about it instead of describing the decade.

Abstract. On 30 July 2026 Ray Dalio described the coming decade for ninety minutes without attaching a probability or a date to any claim, on the stated and defensible ground that bubbles cannot be timed. I take the caution seriously and argue it can be held while still committing to a number. This note sets out the view I committed to. As machine reasoning becomes abundant, the binding constraint migrates to the inputs intelligence cannot manufacture for itself, and almost all of them are permissioned by a utility, a regulator, a commission or a charter. The constraint is therefore a calendar rather than a capital budget, and the rent accrues to the holder of durable access rather than the owner of the best model. The note closes on the distributional consequence and on the pre-registration discipline that makes the view falsifiable.

Keywords: forecasting discipline, pre-registration, permissioned access, AI capital expenditure, interconnection queues, scarcity rent, calibration.

1. Ninety minutes without a number

On the thirtieth of July, Ray Dalio sat down for an hour and a half and described the next decade. Debt service squeezing governments that do not have enough money. An AI boom carrying the classic markings of a bubble. The share of business revenue going to workers falling while the share going to owners rises. American deterrence visibly eroding in Asia. A world order going regional because no single power can hold it any more.

Close to three million people have watched it. It is a serious performance by a serious man, and I do not think he is wrong about much of it.

He also, across those ninety minutes, did not attach a single probability to anything. He did not date a single claim.

That is not an oversight, and it is not laziness. He says plainly that the future is deeply unknown and that even sophisticated investors struggle to time a bubble. Anyone who has tried it knows he is right. But it leaves the listener holding a description rather than a claim, and a description is a strange thing to act on. You cannot be wrong about one. You cannot be scored on one either.

There was a single exception, and it is the most interesting moment in the episode. His host reads him Jeremy Grantham’s view that the peak is, on the historical pattern, very soon. Dalio answers in two words. He’s right.

That is a dated claim. It is the only one in ninety minutes, and he arrives at it by agreeing with somebody else.

I am not scoring a point off that. The instinct behind it is sound, and the honest version of forecasting is much closer to Dalio’s caution than to the confident noise that fills most of the field. But I think the caution can be held while still writing the number down, and I spent this summer trying to do the second thing.

2. What I think the decade turns on

Here is the bet in one sentence. Intelligence is getting cheap and permission is getting scarce.

The first half is uncontroversial now. The cost of a unit of machine reasoning has collapsed and keeps collapsing. Almost everyone I read has priced that in, and the argument has moved on to who captures the value.

The second half is where I part from the consensus. The usual story says that when a technology becomes abundant, the state recedes and the market takes over. I think the opposite is happening. As intelligence becomes abundant, the binding constraint moves to the things intelligence cannot manufacture for itself, and almost all of those things are permissioned by somebody.

An agent can reason. It still needs an energized machine to run on, and a firm megawatt at a particular substation on a particular date is not something you can buy at any price. It needs an eligible chip, and eligibility is now decided in two capitals rather than by a purchase order. It needs a supply chain that clears customs, and it needs an authorized rail to settle payment. Each of those is a permission. Each is granted by a utility, a regulator, a commission or a charter, and each moves on a calendar that no amount of capital compresses.

That is the part I would put in front of anyone who thinks this is a technology story. The constraint is not the capital expenditure. It is the calendar. Turbine order books that stretched from 2028 to 2030 in the space of four months. A median wait from interconnection request to operation of around five years, against under two at the start of the century. Announced nuclear capacity for AI that sounds enormous until you notice that almost none of it is under construction, and that the fastest project anywhere is not a new reactor but the resurrection of an old one, relicensed and still without a firm date to reconnect.

Money can bid for a place in that line. It cannot abolish the line.

If that is right, then the rent from this technology does not accrue to whoever owns the best model. It accrues to whoever holds the durable access, and the interesting question for the next five years is not which lab wins but who is sitting on contracted power, licensed compute, strategic supply and chartered settlement when the abundance arrives.

3. The part that should bother you

A permissioned world is not a neutral one, and the hierarchy has a bottom.

Countries that cannot obtain licensed compute will consume the capability without ever accumulating the capacity, which is a rental relationship dressed up as participation. Food-importing economies will hike into supply shocks that the Federal Reserve is entitled to look through. Ratepayers will finance substations whose option value accrues to somebody else entirely. Entry-level knowledge workers will lose the apprenticeship rung a long time before the profession disappears.

Africa holds something like sixty per cent of the world’s best solar resource and attracts about two per cent of global renewable energy investment. That gap is not a technology problem and it is not a resource problem. It is a permission problem, and it is the same one, seen from underneath.

4. Writing it down

None of the above is worth anything as an opinion. I have read a great many confident essays about this decade and I have watched almost none of their authors return to see how they did.

So I wrote fourteen of these claims down as binary questions, attached a probability to each, named in advance the exact published series that decides yes or no, and published the whole thing before any of it could resolve. Some of them will be wrong. The point is that you will be able to see which ones, on dates I chose before I knew the answer, against sources I do not control.

I am not going to defend the number here. That is what the log is for, and the log will still be there in 2030 whether it flatters me or not.

If you cannot put a number on it, you do not have a differentiated opinion. You have an opinion.

The fourteen forecasts, their resolution criteria and their running score are public in the Forecast Wing. Probabilities were pre-registered and are marked against named third-party series, win or lose.
