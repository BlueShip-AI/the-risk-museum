Working Paper No. 22 · 3 August 2026, revised 19 August · 184 tested, 2 survived, as of 4 August 2026

Who Has To Trade Tomorrow

184 trading ideas judged, two alive, and only 69 of the 184 under this museum’s own gates. The other 115 were judged inside a vendor’s simulator and are labelled as such below. The 182 that died include two published factor libraries and the classic anomaly canon. The survivors share one thing the casualties do not. Somebody is forced to trade against them on a known schedule.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

1. The count

Three hundred and sixteen ideas filed. One hundred and eighty-four actually tested. Two survived.

One hundred and twenty-eight tests errored before producing a verdict: roughly three-quarters blocked on vendor data I could not obtain for free, most of the rest plain compute timeouts. Four are still queued. A test that never ran earns no place in the denominator. The honest pass rate is two of 184, or 1.1%.

2. Three libraries, no survivors

Microsoft publishes a library of 158 technical alphas (rule-based trading signals). I ran them as variants of one idea, judged together. Testing many variants of the same idea means many chances to get lucky, so the required evidence rises with each attempt. Twenty-nine clear a naive t-statistic of 2.5, where t is the standard measure of whether a result is distinguishable from luck. One clears the raised bar, a signal counting the days since the high of the last 20 days, at t = 4.46, which also survives the cost stress test at a t of 3.68. It is then stopped by a 55% collapse in its Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk) from in-sample to out-of-sample, that is, from the data used to build it to data it never saw, against a 30% limit. Zero are promoted, and the hurdle that ends it is out-of-sample decay rather than the multiple-testing correction. Decomposed, the library is reversal at short horizons and a volatility risk premium, expressed 158 different ways. Costs alone do not kill the library: sixteen of the twenty-nine naive hits survive even the stress that halves the gross return and doubles the costs. What kills it is out-of-sample decay and the rising evidence bar.

3. The two that lived

A dealer gamma classifier. Dealers who are long gamma hedge against the move and compress the day's range. Dealers who are short gamma chase it and expand the range. The signal predicts the next day's range, not its direction.

The numbers, because a mechanism without them is a story. Days in the bottom gamma quintile realise 2.59 times the range of days in the top quintile, and the top quintile pins 79.7% of the time against an unconditional pin rate of 61%, a lift of 1.31 rather than the naked 80% a reader might price. Standing alone the signal carries a t of 11.14 across 3,565 trading days, on a regressor built as a 252-day rolling percentile, so the number of independent windows of the regressor's own construction is closer to fourteen than to 3,565 and the t should be read accordingly. The first objection any practitioner raises is that this is volatility wearing a costume, so: gamma correlates with VIX at −0.47, and controlling for VIX the t falls to 7.36 rather than to nothing. In the same regression VIX itself carries a t of 12.3, so the control dominates the signal and gamma is the increment, not the driver. Under the stress applied to this lane it holds at 3.68. Two corrections to how that was previously stated. The ticket was recorded with a family size of one, which is wrong: this shop has judged at least seven dealer-gamma looks, and the bar at n = 7 is t ≥ 3.13 rather than 2.50. The result clears the corrected bar, so the verdict stands, but it stands as an unauditable pass rather than a clean one. And this lane has no cost model, so “stressed” here means the effect halved against the unstressed standard error, which is not the gross-halved-and-costs-doubled rerun that Section 2 describes.

A bubble screen. Names that double inside two years crash more often than a control drawn from the same month, at a rate that clears the bar but only just.

The numbers need their denominator stated, because the two available denominators tell different stories and only one is honest. Counted by name-month, flagged names crash at 23.0% against a 15.1% base, a ratio of 1.51. Counted by episode, which is one flag per run-up and the count the lab itself labels “the honest N”, it is 232 of 1,192, or 19.5% against a 15.2% base. That is a ratio of 1.283 against a gate set at 1.25. The stressed z is 5.34 and the episode-level binomial p is 3.7e-05, not the 2.7e-80 the name-month count produces. The gate was evaluated on name-months; the honest margin is the episode one, and it is thin. The caveat that survives every rerun is episode clustering: run-ups arrive in cohorts, so the effective number of independent observations is far below 1,189 and the p-value is optimistic by an amount I have not bounded. That is why this is described as clearing the bar only just, despite a z that reads comfortable.

Neither is a price factor and neither was clever. Both mechanisms are decades old; the specific published signals date to 2018 and 2019, and both are publicly documented.

4. The question that separates them

The distinction is not positioning against price. I believed that and this month proved it too loose.

Commitments of Traders, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission's (CFTC) report of futures positioning, is the canonical positioning dataset. I tested it three ways against a required t-statistic of 2.87. The best attempt reached a t of 1.24, with the sign the wrong way. Dead on arrival.

Somebody is forced. An options dealer who is short gamma does not decide whether to hedge. The hedge is a mechanical consequence of the book he already holds, on a schedule the market can see. A speculator long S&P futures has an opinion, and an opinion poll with a lag is not a mechanism.

The bubble screen is the weaker version. A crowd that doubled its money is not forced to sell, but the unwind is not optional.

Hayek got there first. A signal derived from price is trying to extract information the price has already absorbed.

The operative question is not what the crowd thinks. It is who has to trade tomorrow whether they want to or not.

5. What this predicts

Corrected 19 August 2026. As first published this section named a list of mechanisms that should keep clearing the bar at a higher rate than anything derived from price alone: dealer hedging, index reconstitution, selling driven by taxes, liquidation driven by redemptions, margin calls. Four of those had already been tested here and rejected before the paper went out, which makes them examples rather than predictions. The index effect died on 24 July, tax-loss harvesting on 13 July at a negative t, the tax-loss-selling bounce on 24 July, and dividend-cut forced selling on 28 July at the margin-of-safety rerun. Levered ETF rebalancing has since joined them, per Section 7. The surviving form of the claim is therefore much weaker: a compelled counterparty on a visible schedule is where I will keep looking, and the record so far is that most such mechanisms die too. Sentiment and survey data should keep failing, because holding an opinion imposes no deadline.

A survey or sentiment signal that clears a bar properly raised for the number of attempts, under a cost stress test, would falsify this. It is cheap to test.

6. What is wrong with this paper

The universe is survivorship biased. It is today's large caps, companies already known to have survived, so raw statistics are upper bounds rather than estimates.

Two survivors is a sample of two. Section 4 is a pattern read off two successes and 182 failures, which is the kind of thing the automated research pipeline behind this site exists to be suspicious of. It is a selection rule for what to test next, not an established finding.

The lanes were not chosen at random. I tested WorldQuant BRAIN and Microsoft Qlib's public library of 158 technical alphas (Alpha158) because they were available, and a different sample could give a different rate.

The survivors have not been re-tested on data that did not exist when they passed. That test matters most and it is scheduled, not done.

7. Update, 19 August 2026: eighty-eight more judged, and a falsification of Section 4

Sections 1 to 6 are a 4 August snapshot. The pipeline kept running, and what it produced since is worse for this paper than the snapshot was. As of 18 August it had judged 272 ideas rather than 184, and as of today 277, with no new trading survivor in either count (one study-lane finding passed on 12 August, the museum’s own forecast-calibration self-test; it is not a tradable signal and does not enter the vault count).

The composition matters more than the total. Of the 88 judged between 4 and 18 August, 79 are WorldQuant BRAIN expressions killed inside that vendor's own post-simulation checks (the other nine: five pairs-family rejections, three compelled-counterparty rejections, and one passed study-lane finding), and they are price signals. Only three tested a compelled-counterparty mechanism, which is the thing Section 4 actually predicts should survive at a higher rate. All three were rejected.

One of those three is the sharpest test this thesis has faced, and it argues against it. Levered ETF end-of-day rebalancing is the purest instance of the definition in Section 4: a leveraged fund must rebalance into the close to maintain its stated exposure, the requirement is mechanical, and the schedule is public. Ticket 2aa66023b9be729f, rejected 14 August at a Newey-West t of −0.03, bootstrap P(Sharpe ≤ 0) of 0.53, and an in-sample to out-of-sample degradation of 701%. A second version scaling the flow by SEC-reported fund assets reached t = 0.89 and also died.

The honest reading of that ticket is narrower than a flat null, and the record says so. The critic's addendum of 14 August records it as a cost death, not a no-effect: gross return +7.5% a year at a diagnostic t of 2.02, destroyed by the margin-of-safety rerun to t = −3.14. So the mechanism leaves a gross footprint and cannot be traded through this engine's cost assumption. That is the same verdict as Working Paper No. 25, arrived at from the opposite direction, and it is a more interesting result than silence would have been.

What it is not is evidence for Section 4. An earlier version of this section claimed the recent window ran in the predicted direction. It does not. Section 4 predicts a differential pass rate, forced-flow mechanisms above price signals. Out of sample the forced-flow arm is three tested and zero passed; the price arm is 84 tested and zero passed. Two zeroes distinguish nothing. On the house standard for this kind of comparison the arms would need to be adjudicated in the hundreds before the difference could speak at all, and the one clean instance that did run went the wrong way. Section 4 remains a selection rule for what to test next. It has now survived nothing.

On the shape of the mortality, an earlier version of this section also read it backwards. Of 274 rejections, 199 carry the stage label brain-checks, which sounds like a cheap pre-screen and is not one: the vendor loop runs a full simulation first and only then reads the pass flags, so those 199 are completed backtests rejected on their results, chiefly low Sharpe, low fitness and self-correlation. Thirty-eight died at this engine's own validation stage and the remaining 37 are scattered. The funnel is therefore a census of one vendor lane under a foreign rulebook rather than a transferable lesson about where to spend compute, and it is reported here only to correct the earlier claim.

Method. All counts read from the pipeline's database of every idea tested here on 4 August 2026, via the read-only query layer, except the sector and industry grid, whose 32 cells were judged inside its own lab artifact and never filed as database rows. Judged means a test reached a verdict of passed, rejected or decayed; errored and queued tests are excluded from every rate. The stack of statistical hurdles in the main automated lane is a Newey-West t-statistic (adjusted so that autocorrelation in returns does not overstate significance) against a bar raised by a Bonferroni correction for the number of variants of the idea, a ten thousand draw block bootstrap (resampling the return series in blocks to see how often chance alone matches the result), an in-sample to out-of-sample degradation limit, and a rerun for margin of safety with the gross return halved and the costs doubled. Studies in the research lane run a subset and say so in their own papers: the studies of 13F crowding (the 13F is the Securities and Exchange Commission form on which large managers disclose US equity holdings each quarter) and disclosure text were judged on a Newey-West t-statistic against a bar raised for the number of attempts, with no bootstrap, no out-of-sample split and no cost model. Claiming the full stack of hurdles applied to all 184 would be false. Every statistic is computed by deterministic code and none by a language model.
