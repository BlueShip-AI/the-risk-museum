Priced Wrong on Purpose: Favourite-Longshot Bias and the
Failure of Two Pre-Registered Hypotheses in Prediction Markets

V. Dimopoulos
BlueShip Research
Working Paper No. 16 · 26 July 2026

In one line: Contracts priced near 35 cents pay out 17% of the time, and two of my three predictions were wrong.

Abstract. I test three pre-registered claims about pricing in prediction markets on 250,858 resolved Kalshi markets spanning 59,748 distinct events. Two are falsified. Prices are not calibrated. The favourite-longshot bias is confirmed and is economically large. My third claim, that order flow concentrated on one side marks crowd error, is falsified in SIGN. I do not promote the bias, because the dataset carries no order book.

Keywords: prediction markets, calibration, favourite-longshot bias, Brier decomposition, pre-registration, order flow.

1. Why this venue

Prediction markets resolve to a known truth, which makes calibration directly measurable rather than inferred. I have a graded record in them, top 6% of Jump Trading's Probability Cup over 620 forecasts at a mean Brier score of 0.231 against a 0.25 coin baseline.

2. Data and method

The source is a public dataset of Kalshi and Polymarket records. I use Kalshi only, a deviation recorded before running anything. Kalshi trade rows carry price, taker side and size as fields of their own and market rows carry the resolution, whereas Polymarket trades need blockchain decode. For each resolved market I take the average price over the final 24 hours of trading, weighted by volume, and require at least five trades in that window. The base rate of YES resolution is 0.381.

3. Results

3.1 Calibration, and the size of the miss

The Murphy decomposition gives Brier 0.0927 = reliability 0.0193 − resolution 0.1617 + uncertainty 0.2358. Prices separate winners from losers. The reliability term is not near zero, and the miss is systematic rather than noisy.

3.2 The favourite-longshot bias

Contracts trading between 0.30 and 0.40 resolve YES 17.0% of the time, so a buyer pays roughly twice what the contract is worth. Contracts trading between 0.60 and 0.70 resolve 87.8% of the time. Longshots are dear and favourites are cheap, monotonically across the book.

3.3 Crowding, falsified in sign

I predicted that markets with taker flow heavily on one side would be worse calibrated, on the reasoning that concentrated flow marks a crowd about to be wrong. The data say the opposite.

The books with flow on one side are the best calibrated of the three groups. Concentrated flow usually marks an outcome that has become obvious, not a crowd in error. My hypothesis had the sign backwards.

4. Robustness

Correlated contracts and intraday churn could explain the bias away, and neither does. Restricting to one market per event, 59,748 independent observations, leaves the bias essentially unchanged at +0.140 in the 0.30 to 0.40 band and −0.214 in the 0.60 to 0.70 band. Splitting by contract duration, the error in the longshot half runs +0.104 to +0.124 and the error in the favourite half −0.138 to −0.162 across every bucket from under six hours to beyond two days.

5. Why this is not promoted

A calibration gap measured on traded prices is a measurement, not an edge. Execution happens against a quoted book, and this dataset contains no order book. I attempted to close this with a commercial history of quotes at the top of the book and found the provider holds no data for any market in this sample. A finding this large on a regulated exchange with professional participants should raise suspicion, not confidence, until that check is made.

A study that confirms everything its author expected is usually measuring the author.
