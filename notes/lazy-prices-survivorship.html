Lazy Prices Meets Survivorship: Why a Disclosure-Text
Anomaly Inverts in a Survivor-Only Sample

I tested 14,155 filings to see if rewritten wording predicts losses. The result flipped, because failed companies were missing.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

Abstract. Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen (2020) show that firms which change the language of their periodic filings subsequently underperform. Disclosure inertia is good news. I test the effect on 14,155 quarterly and annual reports (10-Q and 10-K filings) drawn from the SEC’s Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis and Retrieval system (EDGAR) for 406 large caps currently in the index, measuring text change as one minus the cosine similarity (a standard measure of how much two documents’ wording overlaps) of a filing to the same period a year earlier. The effect does not replicate. The measure is highly sensitive to how it is specified: a naive version compared quarter over quarter is a precise zero (t = 0.13; the t-statistic is the standard measure of whether a result is distinguishable from luck), while a version controlled for season and cleaned of stopwords (common filler words) is significant but opposite in sign. The firms that changed the most slightly outperformed (the spread of low minus high change was −1.97% over the next quarter, t = −2.55 with the Newey–West adjustment for correlated observations), and even that is well below the bar this site sets, after stress testing, for promoting a signal. I argue the inversion is a survivorship artifact. A panel of current constituents deletes the informative short leg (the firms a trader would bet against, those that rewrote their disclosure on the way to trouble), leaving only the changers that survived. The anomaly cannot be cleanly tested without data free of survivorship bias. No signal is promoted.

Keywords: disclosure text, filing similarity, Lazy Prices, survivorship bias, forensic accounting, multiple testing.

1. The anomaly, and why it should be orthogonal

Most published equity signals are functions of price. The Lazy Prices effect is not one of them. It reads the language of a filing. When a firm reproduces last year’s disclosure nearly verbatim, nothing material has changed and the stock does well; when it rewrites, something is being managed, and the stock underperforms. The mechanism is forensic rather than statistical, in the tradition of reading the narrative for what the numbers omit (Mayew, Sethuraman and Venkatachalam, 2015). A signal of this kind, orthogonal (independent of the price-based signals most strategies already trade) and lightly crowded, is exactly what a research program that has exhausted price signals should want to test.

2. Data and method

I harvest every 10-Q and 10-K primary document filed since 2015 by the 406 names in my universe (the set of stocks tested by the automated research pipeline behind this site) with EDGAR coverage (14,155 filings in all) and reduce each to a word vector (a tally of how often each word appears) of stripped text, discarding inline eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL) scaffolding and the frequent stopwords that every filing shares. Following Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen, text change is measured year over year, same period: a 10-Q is compared to the same fiscal quarter a year earlier and a 10-K to the prior year’s 10-K, so that ordinary seasonal differences in disclosure are not counted as change. Each filing’s return, adjusted for the market, is measured over the 63 trading days beginning the day after the filing. Everything is point-in-time (each measure uses only information available on the day it is dated); the universe is current constituents, so it is survivorship biased by construction.

3. Results: fragile, and the wrong way round

The measure does not survive its own robustness checks. Compared quarter over quarter, the spread of low minus high change is an exact zero (t = 0.13). Compared year over year and cleaned of boilerplate, the same spread becomes significant and reverses. The filings that changed least underperform and those that changed most slightly outperform. That is the opposite of the published effect, and a signal whose sign depends on the specification is not a signal one trades.

4. The mechanism: survivorship deletes the short leg

The inversion has a clean explanation. Lazy Prices is, in large part, a result on the short side. The money is in avoiding, or shorting, the firms that rewrite their disclosure because trouble is coming. Those are precisely the firms that underperform, are removed from the index, and in many cases delist. A panel of current constituents has already deleted them. What remains among the heavy changers are the survivors: firms that rewrote their filings while growing, restructuring successfully, or acquiring, and lived to stay in the index. Restricting the sample to survivors keeps the benign changers and discards the malign ones, which is enough to flip the sign of an anomaly that lives on the short side. The result in Table 1 is therefore most likely a measurement of who survived, not of what disclosure change predicts.

5. Verdict

Nothing is promoted. The strongest specification reaches t = −2.55, far short of the bar this site requires after stress testing and deflation for multiple testing (near a raw t of 5; testing several variants of one idea means several chances to get lucky, so the required evidence rises), in the wrong direction, and with a mechanism that points at survivorship rather than information. The honest conclusion is not that Lazy Prices is false (the original result stands on a universe without survivorship bias, which I do not have), but that it cannot be tested here. Reading the language of filings remains a promising orthogonal lane; testing it properly requires point-in-time constituents and delisting returns, the data my pipeline has repeatedly identified as its binding constraint.

The result that a signal inverts under survivorship is worth more than a signal that merely fails. It names the bias precisely and spells out what a clean test would require. A flagged finding is a question, not a conviction; this one asks for better data before it asks for capital.
