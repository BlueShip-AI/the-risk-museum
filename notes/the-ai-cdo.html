The AI-CDO Is Already Being Built

by V. Dimopoulos · Research note #9 · what 2008 says about how AI infrastructure is being financed · July 24, 2026

I want to start by defending securitization, because the honest argument depends on it. Pooling loans and selling claims on the pool is not a crime; it is one of the more useful inventions in finance, and it did not cause 2008. What caused 2008 was four specific, nameable failures that happened to ride inside a securitization. Name them, and you can watch for them anywhere the same machine reassembles. It is reassembling now, around artificial intelligence, and this note is an inventory. It is not a prediction of when, only a description of what.

What is being built

The buildout of AI needs an extraordinary amount of capital, and the most profitable companies in the world have, unusually, started borrowing to fund it because their own cash flow can no longer keep pace. Around that demand a financing complex has grown: data-center developers borrowing against future rents; “neocloud” providers borrowing against multi-year compute contracts; and lenders increasingly willing to take the graphics chips themselves as collateral. The Bank for International Settlements devoted part of its 2026 annual report to this. It is the central banks’ central bank, and it warned about mortgage risk years before 2008. Its language is worth quoting because it is so careful: the sector shows “circular financing,” the “opacity” of the arrangements “compounds vulnerabilities,” collateral is in places “pledged multiple times,” and “signs of stress are already visible.” A chipmaker takes an equity stake in an AI lab; the lab commits to buy that maker’s chips; a cloud provider borrows to house them; an insurer is offered the senior claim on that debt as a safe asset. Money runs in a circle, and each turn of the circle is booked as growth.

The scale is what makes the loop dangerous rather than merely clever. Widely reported estimates put this year’s hyperscaler AI capital spending near three-quarters of a trillion dollars, and the multi-year buildout in the trillions. The money entering from outside the circle, what end users and enterprises actually pay for the output, is smaller by roughly an order of magnitude. So the structure rests on a single bet, that the small outside number grows faster than the large recycled one, and that bet is the whole risk.

The four failures of 2008

Here is the inventory. Securitization was the vehicle; these were the passengers.

  1. The rating was corrupt. Pools of poor mortgages were stamped AAA. Institutions trusted the stamp rather than the mortgage, and the stamp was paid for by the people being rated.
  2. The correlation was hidden. The models assumed one borrower’s default said little about the next. That assumption held in calm and dissolved in the storm: when house prices fell, they fell everywhere at once, and “diversified” pools turned out to be one bet wearing many costumes.
  3. The tail capital was inadequate. The buffers were sized for the normal distribution the models drew, not for the fat-tailed world the market actually lives in.
  4. The incentives were misaligned. Originate-to-distribute: whoever wrote the loan sold it onward and kept none of the risk, so the reward for volume swamped the reward for care.

The same four, in AI clothing

Now hold the AI-credit complex against that list, one line at a time.

Who is grading this paper? Debt backed by data-center rents and depreciating chips is new enough that the rating apparatus for it is young. The 2008 lesson is not “ratings are useless” but “a rating is only worth the independence and the model behind it,” and a young market is exactly where an under-modeled, conveniently generous grade does its damage.

Where is the hidden correlation? This is the part that should keep a risk officer awake. These loans are not independent. They lean, almost all of them, on the continued spending of a handful of hyperscalers and on the fortunes of essentially one chip designer. A diversified-looking pool of data-center loans can be, underneath, a single concentrated bet on whether that spending continues. That is the 2008 correlation trap with fewer moving parts. And there is a hazard mortgages never had: technology obsolescence. A house securing a loan is worth roughly what it was worth last year. A graphics chip securing a loan sits on a steep curve of replacement, and its collateral value can fall for reasons that have nothing to do with the borrower’s health and everything to do with a better chip shipping.

Where does the tail land? Increasingly, on insurance balance sheets. Private credit, including the infrastructure paper this note is about, is being sourced for the long-dated liabilities of annuity books precisely because it yields more than public bonds of the same stated grade. That extra yield is not free; it is payment for the illiquidity and the tail. The question a risk seat has to answer, before the paper is bought and not after, is whether the buffer behind those liabilities is sized for the fat-tailed world or the modeled one.

Are the incentives aligned? Originate-to-distribute is a structure, not an era; it returns wherever the writer of the risk can sell it onward. The honest tell is skin in the game. Does the party that structures the pool keep a real, loss-absorbing piece of it?

So what breaks, and who cashes out

Diagnosis is cheap; the useful question is where the money goes when it goes wrong. To answer it honestly I built a small simulation, deliberately simple and with its assumptions declared. It is not a forecast. Picture the AI-infrastructure debt as a single securitized pool, sliced into three claims that absorb losses from the bottom up. The equity tranche (15% of the pool) is the neocloud, lab, and circular-financing paper. It takes the first loss. The mezzanine (25%) is the GPU- and equipment-backed debt. The senior (60%) is secured by the things that survive a technology cycle: the land, the buildings, the power contracts.

Everything in the pool leans on one number: R, the fraction of the promised AI economics that actually shows up as revenue. I let R range from the bear case (half of what was promised) to the bull case (more than promised), ran two hundred thousand draws, and let the losses waterfall up through the stack. The result is below.

Read across the columns and the whole argument is in one image. The red is loss, filling the stack from the bottom. In the good world (revenue arrives near-promised, the 25th-percentile draw) the equity holders take a haircut and everyone above them is untouched. In the bad world (the median draw, revenue merely disappoints) the equity is wiped out entirely, and who holds that equity matters. Increasingly it is the yield-hungry buyers who were sold the juicy tranche as “diversified AI exposure.” In the ugly world (a 95th-percentile draw, revenue collapses toward half) the equity and the GPU-backed mezzanine are both destroyed and the senior claim finally bleeds, because obsolescence means the chip collateral recovers little, and the circular financing unwinds faster than a single-name default ever could.

Across all two hundred thousand paths the shape is stark: the equity tranche loses 68% of its value on average and is all but wiped in half of them; the mezzanine loses 28%; the senior loses 1% and stays fully intact 93% of the time. That gap is the missing link. It is the reason the big players can build this and sleep. The cash-out is structural. You do not have to bet that AI wins. You earn the origination fees whatever happens; you keep the senior, real-asset-backed claim that survives almost everything; and you sell the equity and mezzanine, the tranches that actually carry the technology risk, to the insurers and pensions reaching for yield. Heads you clip a fee and hold the safe piece; tails someone else holds the GPUs. The only honest question, the 2008 question, is whether the people doing the selling kept a real slice of the loss for themselves.

The scenarios that never happen

A model is only as honest as the myths it refuses. Three that the simulation quietly kills:

  • “GPUs hold value like real estate.” They do not. A building is worth roughly last year’s price; a chip sits on a replacement curve and can lose collateral value the day a better one ships, for reasons that have nothing to do with the borrower. That is why the mezzanine, not the equity, is the tranche that surprises people.
  • “It’s diversified across hundreds of borrowers.” Diversification across borrowers who all depend on the same handful of hyperscalers continuing to spend is not diversification. It is one bet in many costumes, the exact mechanism that turned “uncorrelated” mortgage pools into a single wager in 2008.
  • “The senior tranche is safe, full stop.” Only if it is secured by real assets and not by claims on other claims. A senior slice of circular financing is senior in name and equity in substance. The safety lives in the collateral, not the label.

The saving grace

Here is the hopeful half, and it is real. The thing that makes this not 2008 is sitting in the one variable the whole simulation turns on. A house produces no cash flow; it only ever costs. Compute can produce cash flow, if the applications built on it actually get paid for. The senior claim survives in ninety-three percent of my draws for exactly one reason: revenue has to collapse below roughly sixty percent of what was promised before it bleeds, and sustained revenue at that level is a genuinely different world from the sub-prime one, where the underlying asset could never service anything. The saving grace of the AI boom is revenue. Enterprise adoption, inference demand actually invoiced, applications people renew. That is the water that fills the waterfall from the top and services the stack on the way down. Watch that number rather than the capex headlines. When commentators cheer another hundred billion in datacenter spending, they are describing the size of the promise. The payment has not arrived.

Why this hangs in the museum

Notice what I have not done. I have not called a top, named a date, or told you the AI buildout will fail. On a decade’s view it may well pay off, as the internet did. Timing is a forecast, and forecasts belong in this museum’s Forecast Wing, under resolution criteria, not in an essay. What I have done is take the four things that actually broke in 2008 and show that each one is observable in the AI-credit complex today, then run the stack forward to show the shape of the outcomes and name the single variable that decides between them. That is the whole discipline. 2008 was not a failure to predict the future; it was a failure to describe the present, to say out loud, while the music played, that the diversification was fake, the rating was bought, and the safe tranche was only ever as safe as the revenue underneath it.

The museum’s Blowup Wing hangs the finished version of this story: Long-Term Capital Management, levered against a correlation that held until it didn’t; the mortgage machine that called a concentrated bet a diversified one. Those are plaques because they are over. This note is about a structure that is still being assembled, and the only useful thing to do with a structure that is still being assembled is to name its load-bearing assumptions before they are tested rather than after. The failure of 2008 was never a failure of arithmetic. It was a failure of nerve, the refusal to write down what everyone could already see.
