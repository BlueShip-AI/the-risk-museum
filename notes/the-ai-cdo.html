The AI-CDO Is Already Being Built

by V. Dimopoulos · Research note #9 · what 2008 says about how AI infrastructure is being financed · July 24, 2026

I want to start by defending securitization, because the honest argument depends on it. Pooling loans and selling claims on the pool is not a crime; it is one of the more useful inventions in finance, and it did not cause 2008. What caused 2008 was four specific, nameable failures that happened to ride inside a securitization. Name them, and you can watch for them anywhere the same machine reassembles. It is reassembling now, around artificial intelligence, and this note is an inventory — not a prediction of when, only a description of what.

What is being built

The buildout of AI needs an extraordinary amount of capital, and the most profitable companies in the world have, unusually, started borrowing to fund it because their own cash flow can no longer keep pace. Around that demand a financing complex has grown: data-center developers borrowing against future rents; “neocloud” providers borrowing against multi-year compute contracts; and lenders increasingly willing to take the graphics chips themselves as collateral. The Bank for International Settlements — the central banks’ central bank, and the institution that warned about mortgage risk years before 2008 — devoted part of its 2026 annual report to this. Its language is worth quoting because it is so careful: the sector shows “circular financing,” the “opacity” of the arrangements “compounds vulnerabilities,” collateral is in places “pledged multiple times,” and “signs of stress are already visible.” A chipmaker takes an equity stake in an AI lab; the lab commits to buy that maker’s chips; a cloud provider borrows to house them; an insurer is offered the senior claim on that debt as a safe asset. Money runs in a circle, and each turn of the circle is booked as growth.

The four failures of 2008

Here is the inventory. Securitization was the vehicle; these were the passengers.

  1. The rating was corrupt. Pools of poor mortgages were stamped AAA. The stamp, not the mortgage, was what institutions trusted — and the stamp was paid for by the people being rated.
  2. The correlation was hidden. The models assumed one borrower’s default said little about the next. That assumption held in calm and dissolved in the storm: when house prices fell, they fell everywhere at once, and “diversified” pools turned out to be one bet wearing many costumes.
  3. The tail capital was inadequate. The buffers were sized for the normal distribution the models drew, not for the fat-tailed world the market actually lives in.
  4. The incentives were misaligned. Originate-to-distribute: whoever wrote the loan sold it onward and kept none of the risk, so the reward for volume swamped the reward for care.

The same four, in AI clothing

Now hold the AI-credit complex against that list, one line at a time.

Who is grading this paper? Debt backed by data-center rents and depreciating chips is new enough that the rating apparatus for it is young. The 2008 lesson is not “ratings are useless” but “a rating is only worth the independence and the model behind it,” and a young market is exactly where an under-modeled, conveniently generous grade does its damage.

Where is the hidden correlation? This is the part that should keep a risk officer awake. These loans are not independent. They lean, almost all of them, on the continued spending of a handful of hyperscalers and on the fortunes of essentially one chip designer. A diversified-looking pool of data-center loans can be, underneath, a single concentrated bet on whether that spending continues — the 2008 correlation trap with fewer moving parts, not more. And there is a hazard mortgages never had: technology obsolescence. A house securing a loan is worth roughly what it was worth last year. A graphics chip securing a loan sits on a steep curve of replacement, and its collateral value can fall for reasons that have nothing to do with the borrower’s health and everything to do with a better chip shipping.

Where does the tail land? Increasingly, on insurance balance sheets. Private credit — including the infrastructure paper this note is about — is being sourced for the long-dated liabilities of annuity books precisely because it yields more than public bonds of the same stated grade. That extra yield is not free; it is payment for the illiquidity and the tail. The question a risk seat has to answer, before the paper is bought and not after, is whether the buffer behind those liabilities is sized for the fat-tailed world or the modeled one.

Are the incentives aligned? Originate-to-distribute is a structure, not an era; it returns wherever the writer of the risk can sell it onward. The honest tell is skin in the game — whether the party that structures the pool keeps a real, loss-absorbing piece of it.

Why this hangs in the museum

Notice what I have not done. I have not called a top, named a date, or told you the AI buildout will fail — on a decade’s view it may well pay off, as the internet did. Timing is a forecast, and forecasts belong in this museum’s Forecast Wing, under resolution criteria, not in an essay. What I have done is take the four things that actually broke in 2008 and show that each one is observable in the AI-credit complex today: the grade, the correlation, the tail buffer, the skin in the game. That is the whole discipline. 2008 was not a failure to predict the future; it was a failure to describe the present — to say out loud, while the music played, that the diversification was fake and the rating was bought.

The museum’s Blowup Wing hangs the finished version of this story: Long-Term Capital Management, levered against a correlation that held until it didn’t; the mortgage machine that called a concentrated bet a diversified one. Those are plaques because they are over. This note is about a structure that is still being assembled, and the only useful thing to do with a structure that is still being assembled is to name its load-bearing assumptions before they are tested rather than after. The failure of 2008 was never a failure of arithmetic. It was a failure of nerve — the refusal to write down what everyone could already see.
