Working Paper No. 31 · 14 August 2026 · a measurement of what the quality shift costs

The Junk Didn’t Leave. It Left the Index.

The high yield index really is higher quality: a record 57 to 58% BB, against 38% in 2000. But the spread that pays you for credit risk sits near the floor of its history, the 7% yield is mostly Treasury yield, and the riskiest borrowers did not deleverage. They moved to private credit, where no daily spread prints.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

Abstract. High yield’s marketers are right that the index got safer: BB-rated bonds are now a record 57 to 58% of the US market, against 38% in 2000. They are wrong about what that is worth. The spread investors are paid for credit risk sits near the floor of its history, the "attractive" 7% yield is mostly Treasury yield wearing a credit costume, and the riskiest tier of the index is not tight at all; it is repricing violently. Most importantly, a large part of the quality improvement is a change of address: the riskiest borrowers did not deleverage, they moved to private credit and leveraged loans, where no daily spread prints. Quality is up, compensation is near record lows, and the risk that left the index still exists. It just moved to where you cannot see it.

1. The claim, verified

A recent KKR piece argues high yield has outgrown its junk-bond reputation: 57% BB in the US, 68% in Europe, duration near a 15-year low, call protection as a bonus. The composition numbers check out against independent index data, and the duration claim is conservative: at roughly 3.0 years against a long-run average near 4, it is arguably a record low, not a 15-year one. The facts are right. The conclusion does not follow.

2. What the quality costs

Four numbers, pulled from the Federal Reserve’s public database on 14 August 2026, data as of 13 August, percentiles computed over the freely available three-year window:

SeriesLevelThree-year percentile
High yield index yield7.02%46th
High yield index spread2.71%9th
BB spread1.60%3rd
CCC spread10.24%97th

The yield looks normal. The spread, the only part that pays you for credit risk, is near its floor: 7.02% of yield is 4.31% of Treasury plus 2.71% of credit. Published long-run context says the same thing louder: the index spread’s all-time low is 241 basis points (June 2007) against a long-run median near 450, and BB at 160 sits within about 25 basis points of its all-time record. Meanwhile CCC at 1,024 is at its 97th percentile. The high-quality 57% is priced for perfection; the remnant is repricing. The index average conceals a split market.

3. Where the junk went

The CCC share of the US index fell from a post-crisis peak near 30% to about 10%. Over the same era, private credit grew from roughly $158 billion (2010) to about $2.1 trillion (IMF), and Federal Reserve and BIS research finds private credit borrowers are systematically smaller, more leveraged, and riskier than public-market issuers. Loan data shows the riskiest deals losing syndicated share to private lenders, and the public high yield market itself shrank about 11% from its 2021 peak. One asset manager titled the phenomenon exactly: addition by subtraction. Since 2021 the fallen-angel story has also run in reverse, with rising stars exceeding fallen angels and Ford back in investment grade, so today’s record BB share is sustained by upgrades, BB-heavy issuance, and the quiet exit of the riskiest borrowers to markets without daily marks. The IMF and the Financial Stability Board have both warned about that destination’s opacity.

4. The strongest counterargument, answered

The sophisticated bull case says spreads per year of duration are only modestly rich: about 90 basis points per duration-year today against roughly 100 historically. The arithmetic is correct and partly circular, because duration is this short in part because bonds trade to their call dates at tight spreads; the adjustment uses the symptom to excuse the price. The call-protection benefit itself cannot be verified from public sources, since no index provider publishes the share of the market trading to call. And the analyst who actually tested whether the BB-heavy mix justifies today’s tightness, Marty Fridson, is the named skeptic in this literature, not its cheerleader.

5. What this does not establish

No source, this note included, cleanly separates genuine improvement from migration; both are real, and the 2020 fallen-angel wave genuinely upgraded the index. The percentile table uses the three-year window that is freely reproducible; longer-run anchors are cited published figures. This is a measurement note about compensation, not a forecast, a recommendation, or a view on any fund or firm.
