Working Paper No. 31 · 14 August 2026 · when a benchmark changes composition underneath its own history

A Safer Index Is Not a Safer Market

A record 57 to 58% BB, and the index really is higher quality. It got there partly by losing its worst borrowers to private credit, which means its spread history is not a constant-quality series, and its average hides a monotone gradient: BB is paid at the 11th percentile of three years, B at the 30th, CCC at the 98th.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

Abstract. High yield’s marketers are right that the index got safer: BB-rated bonds are now a record 57 to 58% of the US market, against 38% in 2000 and roughly that level through the pre-Lehman era, so the comparison is with the old normal rather than with one unusual year. They are wrong about what the shift is worth, and a researcher should be wary of the series that records it: an index whose weakest constituents migrate elsewhere does not measure the asset class, it measures what stayed. The spread investors are paid for credit risk sits near the floor of its history, the "attractive" 7% yield is mostly Treasury yield wearing a credit costume, and the riskiest tier of the index is not tight at all; it is repricing violently. Most importantly, a large part of the quality improvement is a change of address: the riskiest borrowers did not deleverage, they moved to private credit and leveraged loans, where no daily spread prints. Quality is up, compensation is near record lows, and the risk that left the index still exists. It just moved to where you cannot see it.

1. The claim, verified

A recent KKR piece argues high yield has outgrown its junk-bond reputation: 57% BB in the US, 68% in Europe, duration near a 15-year low, call protection as a bonus. The composition numbers check out against independent index data, and the duration claim is conservative: at roughly 3.0 years against a long-run average near 4, it is arguably a record low, not a 15-year one. The facts are right. The conclusion does not follow.

2. What the quality costs

Five numbers, pulled from the Federal Reserve’s public database on 19 August 2026, data as of 18 August, percentiles computed over the freely available three-year window. The B row was missing from the version first published, which mattered: B is roughly a third of the index by weight and it is the series that decides whether the “split market” reading holds.

The yield looks normal. The spread, the only part that pays you for credit risk, is near its floor: 7.02% of yield is 4.31% of Treasury plus 2.71% of credit. The three-year window understates this, and I should have said so at publication. A 1996 to 2025 history of the index spread was already cached on disk when this paper went out, and against it the current 2.75% sits at the 4th percentile, not the 17th, with a long-run median of 4.56% and an all-time low of 2.41%. The short window is the conservative presentation, not the aggressive one. The correct reading is that the index spread is nearer its floor than the published table implied. Meanwhile CCC at 1,024 is at its 97th percentile. The high-quality 57% is priced for perfection; the remnant is repricing. What the index average conceals is not a two-sided split but a monotone gradient by quality: 11th percentile at BB, 30th at B, 98th at CCC. That is a cleaner and more demanding result than the two-extreme version first published here, because it holds at every rung rather than only at the ends.

3. Where the junk went

The CCC share of the US index fell from a post-crisis peak near 30% to about 10%. Over the same era, private credit grew from roughly $158 billion (2010) to about $2.1 trillion (IMF), and Federal Reserve and BIS research finds private credit borrowers are systematically smaller, more leveraged, and riskier than public-market issuers. Loan data shows the riskiest deals losing syndicated share to private lenders, and the public high yield market itself shrank about 11% from its 2021 peak. One asset manager titled the phenomenon exactly: addition by subtraction. Since 2021 the fallen-angel story has also run in reverse, with rising stars exceeding fallen angels and Ford back in investment grade, so today’s record BB share is sustained by upgrades, BB-heavy issuance, and the quiet exit of the riskiest borrowers to markets without daily marks. The IMF and the Financial Stability Board have both warned about that destination’s opacity.

4. The strongest counterargument, answered

The sophisticated bull case says spreads per year of duration are only modestly rich: about 90 basis points per duration-year today against roughly 100 historically. The arithmetic is correct and partly circular, because duration is this short in part because bonds trade to their call dates at tight spreads; the adjustment uses the symptom to excuse the price. The call-protection benefit itself cannot be verified from public sources, since no index provider publishes the share of the market trading to call. And the analyst who actually tested whether the BB-heavy mix justifies today’s tightness, Marty Fridson, is the named skeptic in this literature, not its cheerleader.

5. What this does not establish

No source, this note included, cleanly separates genuine improvement from migration; both are real, and the 2020 fallen-angel wave genuinely upgraded the index. The percentile table uses the three-year window that is freely reproducible from FRED. The 1996 to 2025 anchors in section 2 are computed here from a cached daily history of the index spread, not taken from a published source. This is a measurement note about compensation, not a forecast, a recommendation, or a view on any fund or firm.

Method
Spreads and yields from FRED (ICE BofA series BAMLH0A0HYM2, BAMLH0A1HYBB, BAMLH0A2HYB, BAMLH0A3HYC, BAMLH0A0HYM2EY), pulled 19 August 2026 (data as of 18 August). Every percentile in the table is computed deterministically over that three-year window, 786 daily observations from 21 August 2023 to 18 August 2026, because FRED’s own ICE window is three years. The 1996 to 2025 anchors in section 2, the 4th percentile, the 4.56% median and the 2.41% all-time low, are not cited published figures. They are computed here from a cached daily history of BAMLH0A0HYM2 at state/cache/wayback_BAMLH0A0HYM2.csv, 7,530 daily observations from 31 December 1996 to 3 November 2025.

Honesty
No source, this note included, cleanly separates genuine quality improvement from migration; both are real. A measurement note, not a forecast or a view on any fund or firm. A declared control was not run. The ticket behind this paper specified holding BB, B and CCC weights constant so that composition drift could not masquerade as spread compression, which is the paper's own central worry. That control was never executed and is still not run here, because the historical index weights by rating are not in the cache and the free series do not carry them. Every percentile on this page is therefore a changing-composition series, exactly the thing the abstract warns the reader about. The finding survives the gap in direction and is unquantified in magnitude until the control runs.
Revision
19 August 2026. Four corrections. (i) A duplicated clause in the lede, live since publication, is removed. (ii) The B bucket, roughly a third of the index by weight, was missing from the table; adding it turns a two-extreme "split market" into a monotone gradient, 11th percentile at BB, 30th at B, 98th at CCC, which is the stronger claim. (iii) The whole table is recomputed on a single fresh pull dated 18 August rather than adding one row from a different vintage. (iv) The paper cited long-run figures secondhand while a 1996 to 2025 history of the index spread sat cached on disk; used directly it puts the current spread at the 4th percentile against the 17th on the three-year window, so the original presentation was conservative rather than aggressive. No conclusion reversed; two were strengthened.
