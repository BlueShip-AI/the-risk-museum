Where the Edge Was: A Category-Level Decomposition
of 620 Brier-Scored World Cup Forecasts

V. Dimopoulos
BlueShip Research
Working Paper No. 17 · 27 July 2026

In one line: I scored best in small markets nobody prices carefully. In the two biggest I lost ground, and it turns out my forecasts there were fine. The crowd was just better.

Abstract. I decompose by category the 620 settled forecasts of Working Paper No. 5, entered in a competition on the 2026 World Cup and scored by Brier score. The platform’s scale puts Match Outcome at −6.1 on 179 forecasts and Goals and Scoring at −15.9 on 145, on undocumented units, so nothing is claimed beyond the ordering and the sign. Rescored against the best constant forecast in each category, both turn positive on 555 forecasts and match outcome ranks second of nine, which locates the deficit in the strength of the competition rather than in the forecasts.

Keywords: forecasting, Brier score, category decomposition, base rates, prediction markets.

1. Introduction

Working Paper No. 5 reported 620 settled forecasts entered in Jump Trading’s Probability Cup, scored by squared error against the outcome (Brier, 1950). The mean Brier score was 0.2309. Against 0.2466 for a forecaster who answers the realized base rate of 44.2% every time, the edge is 0.0157 a question, and the constant 50% forecast at 0.25 that No. 5 quoted is withdrawn as a benchmark. It is the only number here checked against a second source, reconciling with the published calibration table to within 0.001. It is also not significant. Independent draws would give a t-statistic near 2.2, but fifteen to twenty markets a fixture were priced from one view of the same match, and at a correlation of 0.10 within a fixture the statistic falls to about 1.4.

2. The Category Record

2.1 Where the book scored

Three categories scored highest, on 38 of the 620 forecasts. The platform displays halftime tied at +17.4 on 11, penalty and red card at +10.5 on 12, and team corners at +6.9 on 15. All three settle on how a match is played rather than on who wins it. They were selected on the outcome, and on seven calls the standard error on a win rate is roughly seventeen percentage points.

2.2 Where the book lost

The platform groups the book into four category types, reported below as displayed. It does not state whether the figures are averages per forecast or bucket totals, and neither reading reconciles to the account’s overall figure.

2.3 The same book against a documented benchmark

The settled log carries a stated probability and a Brier score for every forecast, and squaring back recovers each outcome. The benchmark used here is the best constant forecast in each category, its own realized base rate b, which scores b(1 − b). The reconstruction covers 555 of the 620 settled forecasts and assigns categories by question wording.

The two readings disagree, and the disagreement is the finding. Match outcome and goals and scoring are the platform’s two worst categories and are positive here, match outcome second best of the nine. Beating the base rate and beating a crowd that is well informed are separate achievements, and the categories where this book lost ground are the ones where the crowd was sharpest.

2.4 The tilt nobody had to model

Of the 555 reconstructed forecasts, 42.2% resolved yes. The question set was written as thresholds, which resolve no more often than not. A flat 42% forecast, from someone who knows nothing about football, scores 0.2439 against 0.2500 for a flat 50%. That difference, 0.0061 a question, is 44% of this book’s 0.0139 edge over the same benchmark. It was not deliberately taken. The account hedged toward 50%, which Working Paper No. 5 recorded as underconfidence in the 45–55% band.

3. What the Record Does Not Establish

Working Paper No. 5 did not claim that the account out-thought the field, the finish invited it, and the reading is retired here. The contrarian win rate was 48%, below half, on a denominator the platform does not disclose and at a distance from half that no plausible subset size makes significant. Nothing in the record makes departure the source of the edge, and nothing makes it calibration, which the published table decomposes into resolution 0.020 against reliability 0.006.

4. Limitations

This is one tournament, one crowd and one settlement, and the forecasts are not 620 independent draws. The two disclosed calls cannot be fitted by one constant against a consensus benchmark but fit exactly against a benchmark defined as the mean of individual forecasters, and those two constructions imply opposite answers to whether this book beat the consensus, so no claim of skill relative to the consensus is made anywhere here. The counts by category are small, the categories were assigned after the fact, and the markets were chosen by the account, so the mix is itself an output of the process. What can be read from this decomposition is where the account scored, not where an edge exists.

This study is retained because it withdraws a claim the collection published, and then withdraws one of its own. A collection that only ever adds papers is not keeping a ledger.
