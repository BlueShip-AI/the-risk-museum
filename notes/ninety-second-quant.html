Working Paper No. 29 · 13 August 2026 · what happens when deployment is free

The Ninety-Second Quant

The time from a trading idea in plain English to a live deployment on a retail brokerage is now about ninety seconds, in front of tens of millions of accounts. This paper asks what free deployment ships, who funds whom once it does, and names the one measurement that would settle it. Nobody has run that measurement. The data sits inside three or four companies.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

Abstract. In 2026 the time from a trading idea in plain English to a live deployment on a retail brokerage fell to about ninety seconds, and the tools doing it sit in front of tens of millions of accounts. This paper asks what free deployment actually ships. The museum's own receipts answer for the strategy class these platforms generate: real but microscopic information that cannot pay its costs, discovered at industrial scale by users who are never shown a multiplicity-corrected number. New account-level evidence from the full Polymarket ledger (Gomez-Cram, Guo, Jensen and Kung, 2026) shows where the resulting flow goes: 97% of participants trade with no measurable edge, and their losses fund the 3% who persist. The wave does not democratize alpha. It industrializes the noise that pays for it, and it makes the herd itself a tradable and attackable object. The one measurement that would settle the causal chain has not been run by anyone, and this paper says exactly what it is.

1. Deployment went free

The pieces assembled quietly and then all at once. Four companies mark the corners of the new landscape:

  • Robinhood shipped agentic trading in May 2026: a customer’s own large language model agent, connected through an official machine interface, trading a ring-fenced account at the largest retail-facing brokerage to try it.
  • SoFi / Composer: Composer turns a plain-English prompt into a backtested strategy in under a minute, and reported over $200 million in daily trading volume and more than 3,000 community-built strategies last October. SoFi acquired it in June 2026 and put it in front of 14.7 million members, deployable within seconds.
  • QuantConnect, with a community it counts at over half a million, ships an agentic developer tool that writes the strategy code itself for its trading community.
  • Horizon, a venture-backed startup that launched in July, pitches the full loop: a plain-English idea to a live brokerage deployment in about ninety seconds. Its own footer says it is not a broker, a dealer, or an adviser.

None of this is illegal, most of it is well built, and every piece is honest about what it does. The question is what happens when the slowest step in retail quantitative trading, writing and wiring the code, stops being a step at all.

2. What free deployment ships

This museum, the research pipeline I built and publish from, is an unusually clean laboratory for that question, because it runs the same loop as these platforms with one difference: gates. The engine has filed 402 hypotheses and kept 2. The rest died against a bar that rises with every look at the same idea, is stressed at doubled costs and halved returns, and never meets a strategy that was allowed to skip the queue.

Pairs trading, which I closed out here in August after testing it five ways, is the worked example, because it is the exact strategy class the new platforms generate on request. A widely viewed YouTube replication of the classic distance rules reported an 8% annual return with honest inclusions, and its own robustness check showed its best portfolio collapsing from over 7% to under 2% when execution waits a single day. I ran five variants of it, each with my pipeline’s standard one-day execution delay: the information is real, with an information coefficient a formal test confirms, and it is an order of magnitude too small to matter. At pair level the book’s convergence earned roughly 0.3% a year gross against 0.7% in costs. Not a negative edge. An edge smaller than the cost of trading it, findable in ninety seconds, deployable in ninety-one.

That is what the deployment pipelines ship, at scale, to users who see a raw backtest and never a deflated one. The published version of this decay is old news in academia: McLean and Pontiff showed strategy returns fall 26% out of sample and 58% after publication, and the crowding that follows publication is most of the difference. A deployment button compresses that lifecycle from years to weeks.

3. Who funds whom

The sharpest new evidence on where such flow ends up comes from prediction markets, where the account ledger is public. Gomez-Cram, Guo, Jensen and Kung, of London Business School and Yale, ran a skill test over the universe of Polymarket accounts and found that 3% of accounts have persistent, statistically real skill; the other 97% trade with no measurable edge, and their aggregate losses are precisely what funds the skilled minority. Luck does not persist: most accounts that look brilliant in one period flip to losses in the next. Accuracy is real, and it is the wisdom of a paid few, not of the crowd.

Map the agent wave onto that structure and the picture is uncomfortable. The ninety-second pipeline arms the 97% first, because speed without a selection discipline is still noise, just faster. The skilled minority’s actual playbook, reacting first to public information, enforcing the law of one price, betting against behavioral bias, is largely automatable, which means agents will eventually reinforce both sides. But the asymmetry is stark: one side needs discipline that the platforms do not ship, and the other side only needs volume, which the platforms manufacture. Grossman and Stiglitz said markets pay the informed out of the pockets of the uninformed. The agent wave is a subsidy increase.

4. The herd becomes a mechanism

Correlated deployment does more than transfer wealth. Agents built on the same handful of foundation models, prompted with the same vocabulary, reading the same signals, produce correlated books, and simulation studies already find large language model agents exhibit less strategy variance than human traders. The Bank of England warned in 2025 that positions driven by common models and data create procyclical, fire-sale dynamics. Researchers at Google DeepMind (Franklin, Tomasev, Jacobs, Leibo and Osindero) now catalogue “systemic traps”: fabricated signals designed to synchronize homogeneous agents into congestion and cascade, which turns the herd from an accident into an attack surface. A crafted headline that triggers a synchronized sell-off is no longer a thought experiment; it is a published threat category.

There is a number that measures all of this, and it deserves to be better known: the branching ratio of market activity, the fraction of events caused by other events rather than by news, estimable with self-exciting point process models. Filimonov and Sornette measured it rising toward criticality over the algorithmic era. Whether the retail agent wave pushes it further is a measurable question, and to my knowledge nobody has measured it on this wave.

5. The gap in the record

The regulators are watching a different movie. United States investor alerts on artificial intelligence trading are almost entirely fraud-framed: fake bots, fake returns, fake platforms. FINRA’s 2026 Regulatory Oversight Report carries a generative-artificial-intelligence section about firms’ internal use, governance and hallucination risk, and says nothing about retail customers wiring autonomous agents to brokerage accounts, five months before Robinhood shipped exactly that. Crowding, correlated deployment, and signal decay from legitimate mass tools appear nowhere. The academic side asserts the causal chain, consumer platforms to correlated deployments to accelerated decay, and has not measured it, because the platforms’ deployment counts are private and the public proxies are new.

That is the open contribution, stated plainly so someone can take it: match platform-declared strategy counts and deployment dates against the subsequent out-of-sample decay of the signal classes they deploy, the way McLean and Pontiff matched publication dates. The data now exists inside three or four companies.

6. What this does not establish

No causal measurement is presented here; the paper’s point is that none exists yet, and the receipts above are the surrounding evidence, not the proof. The museum’s own numbers are one engine, one universe, daily bars. The herding results on language model agents are simulations, not field data. The branching-ratio rise is the literature’s estimate on earlier eras, not this engine’s measurement of this wave. And the obvious reflexive fact, stated rather than dodged: this museum is itself an artificial intelligence research pipeline. The difference between a ninety-second quant and this shop was never the machinery. It is the bar the machinery must clear, the multiplicity it must confess, and the mortality table it must publish. Those take longer than ninety seconds, which is the whole point.
