Building a Factor Risk Model From Scratch

V. Dimopoulos
BlueShip Research
Working Paper No. 24 · 5 August 2026

In one line: I built a cross-sectional multi-factor equity risk model — the same architecture a commercial risk model uses, not a reproduction of one — and of 16 factors tested on 436 names since 2020, only two clear significance.

Abstract. A factor risk model decomposes a stock’s return into a small number of common drivers plus a residual, and every large risk desk buys one from a vendor whose factor definitions and estimation weights are proprietary. I built the same class of model from public daily price data instead: five style factors and an industry factor per sector, a weighted cross-sectional regression run at every date to extract factor returns, a factor covariance matrix, a specific-risk estimate, and a portfolio-level risk decomposition. Run on 436 US names from 2020 to 2026, two of sixteen factors clear a Newey-West t-stat of 2 on the period — Size and the Energy industry factor — and a diversified 100-name equal-weighted book turns out to be 98% factor risk once specific risk is allowed to diversify away, which is the actual point of building one of these.

Keywords: factor risk model, cross-sectional regression, factor covariance, specific risk, portfolio risk decomposition, Newey-West.

1. Why this, and what it is not

A factor risk model decomposes a stock’s return into a small number of common drivers — size, momentum, industry, and so on — plus whatever is left over, which is the only part that counts as genuine stock selection. Every large risk desk buys one of these off the shelf, and the vendors (MSCI Barra, Qontigo/Axioma) do not publish their factor definitions or estimation weights. That is proprietary, and I have not reproduced it.

What I built instead is the same class of model, from public daily price data, so the mechanics are fully inspectable: five style factors, an industry factor per GICS-style sector bucket, a cross-sectional regression run at every date to extract factor returns, a factor covariance matrix, a specific-risk estimate, and a portfolio-level risk decomposition. It is smaller and cruder than a vendor model on purpose — the point was to build the thing, not to fake the polish.

2. The method, briefly

Exposures. Each stock gets a cross-sectionally z-scored score on five factors, each date, using only information available through that date: Size (log price level, a scale proxy — the panel has no shares-outstanding field, a declared limitation), Beta (252-day rolling beta to SPY), Momentum (12-1 month, skipping the most recent month), Value (a 252-day mean-reversion proxy, not a true book-to-price), and Volatility (60-day realized). Each name also gets one industry dummy, from a sector map that assigns each stock to the sector ETF it correlates with most, rather than a scraped label.

Estimation. At each date, I run a weighted cross-sectional regression of next-day returns on that day’s exposures — market-cap-weighted, ridge-stabilized. This is the vendor model’s actual estimation step: the regression coefficients are the factor returns for that day. Run across many dates, you get a factor return time series — a different object from a Fama-French portfolio-sort factor, since it comes from every name’s exposure simultaneously rather than a long-short quintile spread.

Risk. The covariance of those factor returns is the factor covariance matrix. Specific risk is approximated from each name’s residual variance after removing the average factor effect. Portfolio risk decomposes as σp² = xp′Fxp + w′Δw — factor variance plus specific variance, where xp is the portfolio’s net factor exposure and Δ is the diagonal of specific variances. This is the equation every risk desk actually runs.

Of sixteen factors tested, two clear a t-stat of 2 on this period: Size and the Energy industry factor. Everything else — including Momentum, Value, and Beta, the factors most people assume are reliably priced — sits inside noise on this sample. That is the same discipline the rest of this site runs on, applied to a new instrument: most things that look like a factor are not one, and a model is only useful if it tells you that as readily as it tells you the opposite.

4. The portfolio decomposition, and the actual lesson in it

Running the decomposition on an equal-weighted 100-name book as of 29 July 2026: factor risk is 98.3% of total portfolio variance. Specific risk — the idiosyncratic bet in each individual name — is almost entirely diversified away at 100 names; what is left is nearly pure exposure to Financials, Health Care, Beta, and Technology.

That is not a bug in a small model. It is the actual pitch of a factor risk model, working as intended: once a book is diversified, the manager’s P&L is not coming from stock-picking skill in the way it feels like it is — it is coming from factor exposure the manager may or may not have chosen on purpose. Telling a portfolio manager that is the uncomfortable, useful conversation this entire model exists to have.

This is a demonstration of the architecture, not a production risk model. The specific-risk estimate is a shortcut, not a per-name regression residual. The covariance matrix uses a simple sample estimator — no exponential weighting, no eigenfactor adjustment for small-eigenvalue bias, both of which a vendor model applies and which I would want before trusting this at real capital. The Value factor is a return-based proxy, not fundamental book-to-price. The universe is survivorship-biased, as it is everywhere on this site. None of that is hidden; it is the difference between a working paper and a vendor product, and closing that gap is exactly the kind of work I want to be doing.
