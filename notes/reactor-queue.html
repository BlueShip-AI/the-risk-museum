Nobody Bought a Reactor for Speed.
They Bought a Place in Line.

V. Dimopoulos
BlueShip Research
Working Paper No. 19 · 28 July 2026 · data as of 2026-07-28

In one line: Hyperscalers have announced roughly 15 gigawatts of nuclear power. Fifty megawatts of new build is under construction. A third of the announced megawatts existed before the deals were signed, and the fuel for the advanced half mostly cannot yet be made in America.

A note on credit. The framing of this paper, including the count-of-deals-versus-count-of-construction-sites test and the half-of-one-percent arithmetic, comes from the video essay that seeded it. The framing is theirs. My contribution is verification, correction, extension into fuel and market pricing, and a falsifiable prediction ledger. Most of the video's claims survived diligence. The ones that did not are in Section 4, in full.

1. Summary and abstract

One line. Hyperscalers have announced roughly 13 to 15 GW of nuclear commitments; as of July 28, 2026, exactly 50 MW of hyperscaler-contracted new build is under construction, about 35 percent of the announced megawatts already existed before the deals were signed, and the fuel for the advanced-reactor half mostly cannot yet be made in the United States.

Abstract. I audited every announced nuclear agreement between Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta against primary sources (company releases, NRC dockets, Federal Register, DOE, SEC filings). The ledger sums to roughly 14.7 GW at face value, not the ~10 GW figure in circulation, which traces to an internally inconsistent tracker. The composition matters more than the total. 5,187 MW is existing plant output contractually reallocated to a single buyer. 1,450 MW is restarts of shut plants. 463 MW is uprates. The rest is new-build frameworks, of which 50 MW (Kairos Hermes 2, ground broken April 17, 2026) is physically under construction. I then checked the alternative. The gas turbine queue now runs to 2031 for a new heavy-frame order, so the fast option is not fast either. What every buyer actually purchased, on either fuel, is a position in a queue. I extend the story to the bottleneck the deals do not mention, HALEU enrichment, where licensed US capacity is 900 kg per year against a DOE-stated need above 40 tonnes by 2030. I close with where the market prices the chain and twelve falsifiable predictions, including one that would kill this paper's thesis if it resolves yes.

3. The queue arithmetic. Every shelf is empty.

3.1 The turbine shelf

The obvious rejoinder to slow nuclear is fast gas. The order book says otherwise.

Worldwide gas turbine orders went from 399 units and 58.2 GW in 2024 to 846 units and 100.3 GW in 2025; the US took 427 units, 43.1 GW (EPRI analysis published by Utility Dive, Mar 23, 2026; trade press elsewhere attributes the same counts to McCoy Power Reports, whose primary report I could not access). Scope caveat that circulating versions drop: those are all gas turbines. 70 percent of 2025 units were under 100 MW; advanced H/J-class machines were ~14 percent of units but over half the new MW (same source).

GE Vernova's backlog arc: ~80 GW expected at end-2025 "into 2029" (investor update via Utility Dive, Dec 11, 2025), 100 GW at Q1 2026, 116 GW at Q2 2026, guiding to at least 125 GW by year-end 2026 (GEV Q2 2026 press release, Jul 22, 2026). Composition per Utility Dive's Q2 coverage: 53 GW firm equipment backlog plus 63 GW slot reservations. More than half the headline is reservations, not orders. GEV is already selling 2031 delivery slots and expects 2031 more than half contracted by year-end; on 2032, Strazik said the company needs more time before it can talk about contracting (Utility Dive, Jul 2026). Output ramps to 20 GW per year in Q3 2026, 24 GW in 2028, 30 GW in 2030 (Strazik, Q2 2026 call).

This is not one vendor's problem. Siemens Energy's backlog is ~60 GW, booked out to fiscal 2028, after selling 194 turbines in 2025, nearly double its 2024 total (S&P Global, Jun 30, 2026; Enlit). Mitsubishi Heavy has most slots booked for 2027 and 2028, with orders stretching to 2030 (Bloomberg, May 12, 2026; S&P Global, Jul 2026). Wood Mackenzie's arithmetic closes the case: ~110 GW of 2025 orders against 60 to 70 GW per year of world manufacturing capacity, six-year lead times, prices up 195 percent since 2019 (Wood Mackenzie press release, Apr 1, 2026). The queue grows even at record output. OEMs have told developers to call 7 to 8 years ahead (Power Engineering, Feb 20, 2025; S&P Global put wait times at as much as seven years in May 2025). Full-plant CCGT costs have moved from the $1,116 to $1,427 per kW range for plants scheduled years ago to $2,000+ (GridLab via APPA, Sep 2025), consistent with Wood Mackenzie's 195 percent equipment price rise. Beware units. Dollars per kW circulate on at least two bases: equipment (analyst estimates around $790 heavy-duty, $950 HA-class combined cycle, $1,800 aeroderivative; Utility Dive, Jul 2026) and full plant ($2,000+).

There are side doors, and they prove the thesis rather than refute it. Chevron and Engine No. 1 reserved seven 7HA turbine slots before the panic and announced up to 4 GW of data-center power with in-service originally targeted from end-2027 (S&P Global, Jan 28, 2025), culminating in Project Kilby, a 2.67 GW co-located plant at Pecos, Texas under a 20-year Microsoft PPA, FID expected in 2026 and generation starting 2028 (Chevron release, Jun 22, 2026; CNBC). The deal's speed is the slot reservation. Aeroderivative and refurbished units run roughly 12 to 30 months to power at roughly $1,800 per kW equipment, ~2.3x heavy-duty, proven at scale by Crusoe's 1.2 GW Abilene campus, bridged by 29 GE Vernova LM2500XPRESS packages totaling nearly 1 GW (Data Center Frontier, Feb 25, 2026; GE Vernova). Mobile turbines deliver in months with permitting exposure as the price; xAI ran 46 "temporary-mobile" units at Southaven per Mississippi DEQ before the state permitted 41 permanent turbines on March 10, 2026, and EPA's January 2026 actions closed the mobile-source loophole that made the maneuver possible (Mississippi Today, May 11, 2026; Mississippi Free Press; CNBC, Jan 16, 2026).

Front-door arithmetic for a new campus decided today: turbine slot 2031, first power roughly 2031 to 2033. That is as slow as or slower than the Meta-TerraPower 2032 claim. The fast option is not fast. The scarce asset is the queue position.

3.2 The restart shelf

Hard fact first. As of July 28, 2026, zero US nuclear restarts have delivered a single MWh. Every restart timeline in circulation is a projection.

The whole shelf is three plants: Palisades (~800 to 805 MW), Crane (835 MW), Duane Arnold (601 to 615 MW), together ~2,236 to 2,255 MW, about 2 percent of the ~97 GW US fleet (Utility Dive, Oct 22, 2024). Industry consensus is that essentially nothing else can restart; Oyster Creek, Pilgrim, and Indian Point are too far into decommissioning (Utility Dive, Oct 22, 2024; Manhattan Institute, Nov 2022). This shelf does not restock.

Palisades is the pathfinder and the cautionary tale. Shut May 20, 2022 after ~50 years, sold to Holtec for decommissioning, then reversed. Public support totals roughly $3B, not the $1.52B usually quoted: the DOE loan (of which $491M disbursed as of Sep 16, 2025, per DOE), $1.3B of USDA money to the co-op offtakers Wolverine (435 MW) and Hoosier (369 MW), and $300M from Michigan. The NRC work was genuinely novel; the plant formally returned to operations status on August 25, 2025, a US first from decommissioning status. And the schedule has slipped serially: on track for October 2025, then end of 2025, then early 2026, then grid reconnection by end-February 2026, missed. As of July 28, 2026 there is no announced restart date (ANS, Jul 8, 2026), with more than 5,000 individual work activities remaining after major projects closed out (Holtec, Jul 2, 2026). Fuel, 68 fresh assemblies, has been on site since October 2025 (World Nuclear News, Oct 21, 2025), not loaded as of the last disclosure. CEO Kris Singh expects power this year, ahead of the March 2027 contract obligation (FT interview via ANS).

Crane is the best-run restart and it still nearly lost years to the wires. Announced September 20, 2024 with a 20-year Microsoft PPA at ~$1.6B project cost; accelerated from 2028 to 2027; DOE closed a $1B loan in November 2025. In 2026 a PJM study implied interconnection could slip toward 2031; Constellation fixed it by transferring capacity injection rights from its retired Eddystone site, granted by FERC June 2, 2026. NRC restart authorization is still pending (draft environmental assessment June 8, 2026). Fuel load is planned for Q2 2027.

Duane Arnold, derecho-damaged rather than economically shut, targets early 2029, possibly Q4 2028, at over $1.6B, with the NRC licensing bundle due before January 2028 (ANS; NextEra; NucNet; Iowa Public Radio, Apr 29, 2026).

Working number: 3 to 4+ years from decision to grid for plants that were never gutted, and the nearest-to-finish project has missed every date it named.

Vogtle 3&4: certified 2009 at ~$14B for 2016/2017 operation; first nuclear concrete March 2013; Unit 3 COD July 31, 2023; Unit 4 COD April 29, 2024; over $31B to the owners, ~$35B including the $3.7B Toshiba settlement (AP via GPB; Oglethorpe 8-K). NuScale's CFPP died November 8, 2023, before any concrete, at $89 per MWh with the ~$1.4B DOE cost-share plus assumed IRA benefits already inside the price (NuScale/UAMPS 8-K; UAMPS DCRA).

3.4 What the shelves say together

A new heavy-frame gas order today reaches first power around 2031 to 2033. The restart shelf holds 2.25 GW total and its pathfinder has missed four dates. New-build nuclear promises 2030 to 2032 at costs between $6,500 and $20,000 per kW depending on which history you believe. Nobody in the Section 2 ledger bought speed, because speed was not for sale. They bought positions in queues, and the Kilby transaction shows the queue position itself is now the tradable asset. That is the paper's thesis, and it was the video's before it was mine.

4. What the video got wrong, and what I could not verify

This section is required by house rules. Corrections first, plainly.

  1. "About 10 GW" is wrong-low. The likely source is a tracker showing 13 deals and 9.8 GW that lists MW as "TBD" for rows it still counts (AWS-Talen 1,920, Meta-Vistra 2,609, Meta-Clinton 1,121) and omits Duane Arnold (615) and Elementl (1,800) entirely. Worse for the "13 deals among the big four" framing: the tracker's 13 deals span seven buyers, including Equinix, Last Energy, and Aalo, so its deal count and the big-four framing do not describe the same set. Honest total: ~13.4 GW firm-ish, ~14.7 GW at face value. Carnegie independently reaches "up to 13 GW."
  2. "Palisades got its operating license back in July 2025" is wrong as stated. The license never lapsed; it runs to March 24, 2031.   3. Vogtle Unit 4's COD is April 29, 2024, not May 2024 (Oglethorpe 8-K; EIA).
  4. "Vogtle construction from 2009" is imprecise. 2009 was certification and site work; the COL came February 2012; first safety-related nuclear concrete March 2013.
  5. The 846 turbines are all gas turbines, not heavy-duty units. 70 percent were under 100 MW; H/J-class was ~14 percent of units but over half of MW.
  6. The 80 GW GE Vernova backlog was stale on arrival. 116 GW at Q2 2026, at least 125 GW guided by year-end, and more than half the number is slot reservations, not firm orders.
  7. "Only two projects have physical work" is true only for the hyperscaler-contracted set. Crane and Hermes 2, correct. But the same vendors have four other reactors physically underway on non-hyperscaler units (Hermes 1, Kemmerer, Oklo INL, Darlington), and Duane Arnold sits in between with major equipment ordered.
  8. The half-of-one-percent arithmetic survives strengthening. 50/9,800 = 0.51 percent on the video's denominator; 0.34 percent on my larger one. The video's point gets stronger, not weaker.
  9. Crane's capacity is 835 MW in Constellation's materials and 837 MWe in World Nuclear News. My own diligence reports conflict. I use 835 and flag it.
  10. Duane Arnold's buyer is Google. One NucNet URL slug says Amazon; the headline and NextEra's own release say Google.
  11. Meta-TerraPower's "4 GW" includes 1.2 GW of salt-storage boost. Baseload is 2.8 GW.
  12. If Barakah is cited as the on-time counterexample, it isn't. Unit 1 was ~4 years late. The honest version is four units in ~12 years at roughly 3x cheaper per kW than Vogtle.
  13. Oracle has no nuclear deal, and OpenAI has no direct one, as of this search. Absence of evidence, stated as such.

5. The bottleneck the story misses

The video argues construction lags contracts. It does. But the deeper constraint sits upstream, in fuel, and almost none of the deal coverage mentions it.

Most of the contracted new-build fleet (Natrium, Xe-100, Hermes) burns HALEU, uranium enriched to just under 20 percent. One company holds an NRC license to make it commercially in the US. Centrus produces 900 kg per year at Piketon under DOE contract, with cumulative lifetime production above 1,900 kg (DOE, Jun 2025; IPFM, Jul 2026). DOE's own statement of need is more than 40 tonnes by 2030 (energy.gov HALEU program page). One Natrium first core alone is reported at 15 to 20 tonnes (trade press; unverified in a TerraPower primary source). Today's entire US output is under 6 percent of one first core per year. Meta's eight Natrium units imply roughly 120 to 160 tonnes of first cores.

The fixes are funded and late by construction's own clock. DOE's $2.7B package (January 2026, finalized July 2026) buys Centrus expansion to 12 tonnes per year with first new capacity in 2029, Orano's LEU plant for the existing fleet in the 2030s, and General Matter at Paducah. TRISO-X's fabrication plant, licensed February 13, 2026, starts making fuel in the first half of 2028. TerraPower's hedge is a loan to ASP Isotopes to build an enrichment plant at Pelindaba, South Africa, supply promised "2027/2028," plant unbuilt. A US flagship reactor's fuel security resting on a customer-financed, not-yet-built South African facility is a finding in itself. Precedent already exists: in December 2022 TerraPower delayed Natrium two years and named fuel, not construction, as the reason (CNBC, Dec 16, 2022). The only advanced reactor under construction slipped first on fuel.

The conventional cycle has its own cliff. The Russian import ban took effect August 11, 2024; DOE waivers are legal only until January 1, 2028 (H.R.1042). In 2024, US utilities bought 15.0M SWU at an average $97.66; only 19 percent was US-origin and Russia supplied roughly 20 percent (EIA Uranium Marketing Annual Report, 2024 data). Rosatom holds ~44 percent of world enrichment capacity (WNA). Urenco's Eunice plant, the only operating commercial enrichment facility in the US, covers about a third of US LWR needs; its major new capacity arrives 2032 to 2036. SWU spot went from ~$56 in 2021 to a reported $188 to $190 in 2025 (secondary sources; UxC primary is paywalled). Conversion went from ~$6 per kgU in 2016 to $64 to $66 spot (Nuclear Engineering International, Sep 2025). Restarts are the exception that proves the rule; they burn ordinary LEU, and Palisades' fuel was among its earliest completed items, not its delay.

Two more choke points surfaced in diligence. Generator step-up transformers run ~144 weeks, large power transformers ~128 weeks average, up to 4 years for large HV units (IndustrialSage; POWER; Wood Mackenzie). And interconnection can eat years even with a signed PPA and an existing plant; Crane's 2031 scare was resolved only by a FERC waiver moving retired-plant injection rights (Jun 2, 2026). On the builders' own dates, the reactors of 2030 to 2032 meet fuel plants of 2028 to 2036. The queue behind the queue is longer than the queue.

The scarcity rent itself is administratively capped. GEV is up 51 percent year to date in a falling sector at 44x forward earnings, which is itself a 2030s-delivery price on a queue, only one where the deposits are cash today. And the private queue positions are coming to market near the top of the narrative: Holtec has S-1 paperwork on file with the SEC and is planning an IPO at a reported ~$10B valuation (ANS, Jul 8, 2026), and X-energy already went. 7. The Prediction Ledger

Twelve predictions. Each has a binding resolution criterion and a base-rate band with one sentence of reasoning. I assign no probabilities. Pricing is the curator's human-only gate, same as the Forecast Wing.

P1. Palisades synchronizes to the grid on or before December 31, 2026. Resolves YES if an NRC power reactor status report or a Holtec announcement documents first grid synchronization by that date. Resolve by January 15, 2027. Base-rate band: 45–70%. Commissioning programs that have closed out major systems with fuel on site usually finish within one or two further slips, and this one has already burned four named dates. Pricing: [curator].

P2. Palisades delivers contracted power to Wolverine and Hoosier on or before March 31, 2027. Resolves YES on a Holtec, Wolverine, or Hoosier confirmation of commercial deliveries under the PPAs by the contract obligation date. Resolve by April 30, 2027. Base-rate band: 55–80%. This adds a quarter of buffer to P1 and matches the obligation the CEO himself keeps citing. Pricing: [curator].

P3. Crane synchronizes to the grid on or before December 31, 2027. Resolves YES if Constellation or NRC records show first grid synchronization by that date. Resolve by January 15, 2028. Base-rate band: 40–65%. NRC restart authorization was still pending in July 2026 and the only reference-class plant missed every date it named, but Crane was never defueled into decommissioning and has hit milestones early. Pricing: [curator].

P4. Grid-connected SMR capacity in the US and Canada totals at least 300 MWe by December 31, 2030. Definition: an SMR here is a reactor unit of 345 MWe design capacity or less, first synchronized to a grid after January 1, 2027. Resolves YES if IAEA PRIS, CNSC, or NRC records show such units summing to at least 300 MWe net grid-connected in the US or Canada by December 31, 2030. Resolve by January 31, 2031. Base-rate band: 30–55%. This effectively requires Darlington 1 (300 MWe, target end-2030) on time or two smaller units early, against a FOAK base rate of one-to-three-year slips. Pricing: [curator].

P5. At least one MWh is delivered to a grid or buyer from a hyperscaler-contracted new-build SMR by December 31, 2030. Resolves YES on TVA/Kairos/Google, Amazon, or Meta project documentation (or EIA-923 data) showing generation from Hermes 2, Cascade, a Meta-TerraPower unit, or Meta-Oklo Pike County. Resolve by March 1, 2031. Base-rate band: 15–40%. Only Hermes 2 even targets 2030, and its simpler non-power sibling will not finish construction until about 2028 on a permit extended to April 2029. Pricing: [curator].

P6. GE Vernova reports gas equipment backlog plus slot reservations of at least 125 GW for year-end 2026. Resolves YES if GEV's Q4 2026 earnings release states a combined figure ≥125 GW. Resolve by February 28, 2027. Base-rate band: 70–90%. Management re-raised to "at least 125 GW" mid-year after adding 16 GW in one quarter, and reiterated same-year guidance of this kind is usually met. Pricing: [curator].

P7. GE Vernova is publicly taking reservations or orders for 2032 turbine delivery by its Q2 2027 report. Resolves YES if a GEV release or call transcript through August 31, 2027 states 2032 slots are being sold or reserved. Resolve by August 31, 2027. Base-rate band: 60–85%. Orders ran ~110 GW in 2025 against 60 to 70 GW per year of world manufacturing capacity, so the queue lengthens mechanically unless demand collapses. Pricing: [curator].

P8. New capacity actually delivering under big-four nuclear contracts totals less than 3,000 MW by December 31, 2030. Definition: restarts, uprates, and new build delivering power under the Section 2 ledger, excluding reallocated existing output. Resolves YES if company COD announcements and EIA-860/923 data sum below 3,000 MW at that date. Resolve by March 1, 2031. Base-rate band: 75–95%. The maximum plausible on-schedule sum is ~1,963 MW (Crane 835, Duane Arnold 615, uprates 463, Hermes 2 50), so exceeding 3,000 requires multiple 2032-targeted units to arrive years early, against a base rate of zero early FOAK nuclear. A NO here would cut directly against this paper's core claim. Pricing: [curator].

P9. NRC-licensed US commercial HALEU production reaches at least 5 tonnes in calendar 2029. Resolves YES if DOE announcements or Centrus's 10-K covering 2029 document ≥5,000 kg of HALEU produced in the calendar year. Resolve by March 31, 2030. Base-rate band: 25–50%. P10. TRISO-X's TX-1 plant begins commercial fuel fabrication by June 30, 2028. Resolves YES on an X-energy or NRC confirmation that commercial fabrication operations have begun by that date, matching the company's stated first-half-2028 target. Resolve by July 31, 2028. Base-rate band: 35–60%. The license arrived early, but first-of-a-kind fuel plants carry their own commissioning tail. Pricing: [curator].

Base-rate band: 25–50%. The JV itself promised an FID package by 2028, but today's structure is a nonbinding MOU and promised nuclear FIDs slip more often than they hold. Pricing: [curator].

P12. A hyperscaler-contracted new-build reactor delivers grid power by December 31, 2028. Resolves YES if any new-build unit under a Microsoft, Google, Amazon, or Meta contract (not a restart, not an uprate) is documented delivering power by that date. Resolve by March 1, 2029.
