Nobody Bought a Reactor for Speed.
They Bought a Place in Line.

Hyperscalers have announced roughly 15 gigawatts of nuclear power. Fifty megawatts of new build is under construction. A third of the announced megawatts existed before the deals were signed, and the fuel for the advanced half mostly cannot yet be made in America.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

A note on credit. The framing of this paper, including the test that counts deals against construction sites and the arithmetic of half of one percent, comes from the video essay that seeded it. The verification, the extensions, and the prediction ledger are mine.

1. Abstract

I audited every announced nuclear agreement between Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta against primary sources. The ledger sums to roughly 14.7 gigawatts (GW) at face value, and its composition matters more than its total. Every buyer purchased a position in a queue, on gas as much as on nuclear. Upstream sits high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) enrichment, where licensed US capacity is 900 kg per year against a US Department of Energy (DOE) statement of need above 40 tonnes by 2030.

My totals are ~13.4 GW excluding the weakest structures and ~14.7 GW at face value. Carnegie Endowment independently gets "up to 13 GW" (Jun 2, 2026). About a third of the headline is power that already existed. The buyer changed. The grid did not.

3. The queue arithmetic. Every shelf is empty.

3.1 The turbine shelf

The rejoinder to slow nuclear is fast gas. GE Vernova's backlog reached 116 GW at Q2 2026, of which 53 GW is firm equipment backlog and 63 GW is slot reservations, and it is already selling 2031 delivery slots. Wood Mackenzie counts ~110 GW of 2025 orders against 60 to 70 GW per year of world manufacturing capacity, with prices up 195 percent since 2019. A campus decided today gets a 2031 slot and first power around 2031 to 2033.

3.2 The restart shelf

As of July 28, 2026, zero US nuclear restarts have delivered a single megawatt hour (MWh). The shelf is three plants, Palisades (~800 to 805 megawatts, MW), Crane (835 MW), and Duane Arnold (601 to 615 MW), about 2 percent of the ~97 GW US fleet, and it does not restock. Palisades returned to operations status on August 25, 2025, has missed four named dates since, and still has no announced restart date (American Nuclear Society, Jul 8, 2026). The working number is 3 to 4+ years from decision to grid for plants that were never gutted.

My realistic estimate for small modular reactor (SMR) MW on any grid by the end of 2030 is roughly 300 to 425 MWe (megawatts of electric output), essentially none of it contracted by a hyperscaler.

3.4 What the shelves say together

Nobody in the Section 2 ledger bought speed, because speed was not for sale. They bought positions in queues. Project Kilby, Chevron's 2.67 GW plant built on 7HA turbine slots reserved before the panic and sold to Microsoft on a PPA running 20 years, shows the queue position is now the tradable asset.

4. Corrections and open items

  1. "About 10 GW" is wrong on the low side. The likely source is a tracker showing 13 deals and 9.8 GW that lists MW as "TBD" for rows it still counts and omits Duane Arnold (615) and Elementl (1,800).
  2. "Palisades got its operating license back in July 2025" is wrong as stated. The license never lapsed; it runs to March 24, 2031. July 24, 2025 was the transfer of operating authority. August 25, 2025 was the return to operations status.
  3. The arithmetic of half of one percent survives strengthening. 50/9,800 = 0.51 percent on the video's denominator; 0.34 percent on my larger one.

Carried openly, the things I could not verify. HALEU tonnage for the Natrium first core is trade press only. I could not confirm whether Palisades fuel load has begun, could not learn the identity of its fuel fabricator, and could not identify a catalyst for the down session across the sector on July 28, 2026. Crane's capacity is 835 MW in Constellation's materials and 837 MWe in World Nuclear News. I use 835 and flag it.

5. The bottleneck the story misses

The deeper constraint sits upstream, in fuel. Most of the fleet contracted for new build (Natrium, Xe-100, Hermes) burns HALEU. Centrus, the only company licensed to make it commercially in the US, produces 900 kg per year at Piketon against a DOE statement of need above 40 tonnes by 2030. One Natrium first core alone is reported at 15 to 20 tonnes.

The fixes are late by construction's own clock. DOE's $2.7B package buys Centrus expansion to 12 tonnes per year with first new capacity in 2029. TRISO-X's plant, licensed February 13, 2026, starts making fuel in the first half of 2028. The queue behind the queue is longer than the queue.

External read, 8 August 2026. McKinsey’s Electric Power and Natural Gas practice published its own answer to the overbuild question in July 2026 and landed on the same asymmetry this paper argues: a short-term shortfall is likely, and for power players the greater near-term risk is underbuilding. Their arithmetic: data centers are roughly 75% of expected US power demand growth over the next decade, being built at a rate near 30 gigawatts a year; against roughly 120 gigawatts of projected demand growth the grid holds about 40 gigawatts of dispatchable headroom and about 100 gigawatts committed to be built, while 50 to 75 gigawatts of coal and steam-gas capacity retires, for a nationwide capacity need of 30 to 55 gigawatts by 2030. Their gigawatt-accounting sidebar makes this paper’s own point about counting: 500 megawatts of IT load can mean 900 megawatts of interconnection served by 1,000 megawatts of gas nameplate. A consultancy and this site counted with different tools and arrived at the same empty shelf. Source: Tai, Noffsinger and DeFabrizio, Powering AI: How real is the risk of overbuilding?, McKinsey, July 2026.
External read, 13 August 2026. Allianz Commercial, underwriting the buildout rather than forecasting it, published its own constraint map and the mismatch this paper predicts is now a claims category: a large data center builds in 18 to 24 months while the grid to feed it takes years, key grid equipment runs ~30% short with switchgear lead times to 80 weeks and transformers to 50, and local opposition delayed or blocked at least 75 US projects worth roughly $130bn in the first quarter of 2026 alone. The insurer’s summary of who wins: those who can deploy fastest, not simply finance it. Source: The data center construction boom: risks and claims trends, Allianz Commercial, 2026.
External read, 14 August 2026. A 50-state policy survey (Bommarito and Bommarito, SSRN, June 2026) supplies the pipeline denominator this ledger lacked: 890 announced US projects, roughly $1.79 trillion of announced investment, and about 240 gigawatts of reported capacity among disclosing projects. Set 240 announced gigawatts against the build rate near 30 a year and the grid constraints above, and the gap IS the thesis: either announcements deflate through cancellation, the fate of $130bn of projects in one quarter already, or the constraint binds harder than consensus assumes. Both branches favor the asymmetry this paper registered. Announced is not built; China’s 20 to 30% utilization is the cautionary case for the difference.

7. The prediction ledger

Twelve predictions with binding dates and bands from base rates. I assign no probabilities. Pricing them is a human decision, kept outside the automated research pipeline behind this site.

  • P1. Palisades synchronizes to the grid on or before December 31, 2026. 45 to 70%.
  • P2. Palisades delivers contracted power to Wolverine and Hoosier by March 31, 2027. 55 to 80%.
  • P3. Crane synchronizes to the grid on or before December 31, 2027. 40 to 65%.
  • P4. US and Canadian capacity connected to the grid from units of 345 MWe or less, first synchronized after January 1, 2027, reaches at least 300 MWe by December 31, 2030. 30 to 55%.
  • P5. At least one MWh is delivered by December 31, 2030 from a new SMR built under a hyperscaler contract. 15 to 40%.
  • P6. GE Vernova reports gas equipment backlog plus slot reservations of at least 125 GW for the end of 2026. 70 to 90%.
  • P7. GE Vernova is publicly taking orders for 2032 turbine delivery by its Q2 2027 report. 60 to 85%.
  • P8. New capacity actually delivering under the nuclear contracts of the big four, excluding reallocated existing output, totals less than 3,000 MW by December 31, 2030. 75 to 95%.
  • P9. US commercial HALEU production licensed by the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) reaches at least 5 tonnes in calendar 2029. 25 to 50%.
  • P10. TRISO-X's TX-1 plant begins commercial fuel fabrication by June 30, 2028. 35 to 60%.
  • P11. A binding final investment decision to complete V.C. Summer 2&3 is announced by December 31, 2028. 25 to 50%.
  • P12. A new reactor built under a hyperscaler contract delivers grid power by December 31, 2028. 2 to 10%. This is the thesis falsifier. If it resolves yes, speed was real and this paper was wrong.
