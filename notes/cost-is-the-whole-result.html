Working Paper No. 25 · 3 August 2026 · a microstructure transfer, and a boundary

Cost Is the Whole Result

A paper from CFM (Capital Fund Management) argues that trend at short horizons died because the market makers on the other side changed business models, and that tick size normalised by volatility decides which markets kept the effect. I transferred the test to daily US equities in a week. The predicted contrast replicated in direction at a t of 3.21 and died at a stressed 1.64.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

1. Why a shop that runs on daily bars is reading a microstructure paper

In July 2026, Kurth, Eisler, Rej and Bouchaud of CFM (Capital Fund Management, a Paris-based quantitative hedge fund) presented "Is Trend Still Your Friend? A Microstructural Account of the Demise of Short-Term Trend-Following." Fast trend following went flat around 2009. The paper rejects crowding and proposes a change of counterparty. Market makers who warehoused inventory were replaced by high-frequency trading (HFT) firms on intraday mandates to hold inventory flat, which will not absorb persistent directional flow from commodity trading advisors (CTAs). The discriminant is tick size normalised by volatility (tick size is the minimum price increment an exchange allows). Trend survived in markets with large ticks and died in markets with small ones.

The automated research pipeline behind this site tests signals at macro horizons on daily bars (price data summarized once per day), so what kills my results may live in a resolution I cannot see.

2. The transfer, and the caveat I declared before running it

US large caps all tick at a penny, so tick size does not vary. The ratio of tick to dollar volatility does.

Ranking the panel monthly by that ratio produces two tiers, median annualised volatility 29.3% in the tier with the high ratio and 35.5% in the low.

I wrote the fatal caveat into the registered hypothesis before running anything: the paper's mechanism is intraday futures microstructure and this is a daily-bar equity transfer, so a clean failure is expected. Four tests were declared, continuation and reversal in each tier. Testing several variants of one idea means several chances to get lucky, so the required evidence rises with each attempt; here, four declared tests set the bar at 2.96 on the t-statistic, the standard measure of whether a result is distinguishable from luck.

3. What happened

All four declared tests failed. The best reached a t of 0.10.

Declared look NW t Stressed t Bar
Continuation, high ratio −3.40 −4.86 2.96
Continuation, low ratio −5.72 −5.95 2.96
Reversal, high ratio −1.33 −6.95 2.96
Reversal, low ratio 0.10 −6.39 2.96

The four tests are not the paper's prediction, which is a contrast: continuation relatively stronger in the tier with the dense grid, charged nothing extra because both tests were already declared.

The contrast produced +6.14% a year, a Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk) of 0.66, and a Newey-West t of 3.21, the Newey-West adjustment keeping correlated returns from overstating the evidence, clearing the raised bar of 2.96 in the direction the paper predicts.

The rerun for margin of safety took it apart. Halve the gross, double the costs, and the t falls to 1.64 against the base bar of 2.50. Sharpe degradation from in-sample to out-of-sample (on data the model never saw) is 80%, 0.89 to 0.18. Turnover runs about 100x a year, so cost is the whole result.

The arithmetic, since a reader should be able to check it rather than trust it. The house cost assumption is 5 basis points per unit of turnover, the engine's backtester default, plus 25 basis points annualised borrow on the short leg while it is open. At roughly 100 turns a year that is about 5% a year of cost in the base case and about 10% under the doubled-cost stress, against a contrast producing 6.14% a year. There is no version of this where the cost line is a detail. It is the position.

Rejected. A result that only exists at zero cost is not a strategy.

4. What I take from a rejection

The direction replicated, days after publication, on a variable that had never been in my pipeline. The tradeable version did not survive, and the honest reading is not that the paper is wrong. Its mechanism lives at a horizon where my costs are fatal and where daily bars record nothing.

5. The boundary, stated precisely

What I can test on daily bars: cross-sectional signals rebalanced daily or slower; conditioning variables constructed from price, volume, filings, and options open interest; realistic cost sensitivity through a stressed rerun; whether an effect concentrates in a handful of names or a single regime.

What I cannot test, and do not pretend to: order book dynamics, queue position, adverse selection at the tick, the shape of impact within a day, or anything that requires knowing what happened between the close and the open. My cost model is an assumption in basis points on turnover with a doubling stress, a discipline rather than an execution model. A real slippage model needs tick data and a venue map, and I have neither.

What this rejection tells me about that boundary: the one result derived from microstructure that I could get to the raised bar died on turnover, the term a cost assumption built on daily bars estimates worst. Signals whose edge lives closest to the trading day are the ones I should trust least.

6. What would change my mind

The cheapest falsification: if only turnover kills the contrast, slowing the tier rebalance from monthly to quarterly should preserve most of the signal and cut most of the cost. That test is not run. I am naming it rather than doing it, and this note is worth less than it would be if I had.

The second test I have not run, and the one a practitioner asks first: is the contrast orthogonal to anything already known? A tier split on tick over dollar volatility sorts partly on price and partly on volatility, and short-horizon reversal plus a volatility risk premium would reproduce a good deal of it. Nothing here regresses the contrast on standard factors, so I cannot claim it is a new exposure rather than an old one in different packaging. Because the result died on cost anyway the question is academic for this book, and load-bearing for anyone who reads the direction as encouraging. It is named, not answered.

Method. Panel of current US large caps, daily adjusted closes. Ratio defined monthly as tick over the product of price and return volatility over the trailing 63 days, ranked across the cross section and split at the median. Portfolios are decile long-short within tier (long the top tenth of names, short the bottom tenth, ranked by the signal), weighted equally, lagged by one day, rebalanced monthly through my pipeline's standard backtester. Statistics by Newey-West, with the bar raised to account for four declared tests; the stressed series halves gross and doubles costs per my standard construction, where the base cost is 5 basis points per unit of turnover (the backtester's default) plus 25 basis points annualised borrow on the short leg. The universe is survivorship biased, so every raw figure is an upper bound. Source paper: Kurth, Eisler, Rej and Bouchaud, CFM, July 2026.
