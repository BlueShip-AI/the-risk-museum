Working Paper No. 35 · 19 August 2026 · checking a table that is being passed around

The Permissioned Balance Sheet

Private equity firms really are buying life insurers, and policyholder assets really are moving into private credit. The table circulating about it is wrong in most of its particulars: of 104 claims checked against filings, 27 are contradicted and 5 cannot be verified at all. Here is the corrected version, and the three official numbers that carry the argument instead.

V. Dimopoulos  ·  BlueShip Research

Abstract. A table has been circulating that lists thirteen private equity and private credit firms alongside the insurance companies they own, with the implication that policyholder money is being routed into the owners’ own products. The underlying phenomenon is real, large, and worth understanding. The table is wrong in most of its particulars. Of 104 specific claims checked against SEC filings, statutory statements and NAIC reports, 46 are confirmed, 27 are contradicted, 26 are partly right and 5 cannot be verified from any public source. The corrected table is this note’s central exhibit, and it tells a more interesting story than the original. The connection to earlier work is direct: Working Paper No. 31 found that credit risk did not leave the high-yield market so much as change address, migrating to private credit where no daily spread prints. This note is about who bought it.

1. What is actually true, and how big it is

Three official-sector numbers carry the argument, and none of them is the one in the circulating table.

The NAIC counts 137 private-equity-owned US insurers at year-end 2024, up from 90 in 2018 and 139 by June 2025. They held $704.3 billion in cash and invested assets, which is 7.8 per cent of the roughly $9 trillion US insurance industry. Around 96 per cent of that sits in life companies. The Financial Stability Oversight Council repeats these figures.

The table’s footnote claims roughly 25 per cent of US insurers are private-equity backed. On assets that overstates the phenomenon by about a factor of three. The honest version is still striking: a category that barely existed in 2010 now holds the better part of a trillion dollars of policyholder assets, and it has grown by half in six years.

Researchers at the Chicago Fed put life insurer investments in private credit at $849 billion in 2024, more than double the 2014 figure. This is the number that matters, because it measures the behaviour rather than the ownership label. An insurer does not need a private equity owner to hold private credit, and the regulatory concern is about what is on the balance sheet.

The International Association of Insurance Supervisors judges the risk contained for now. Its assessment is that limited exposure to alternative assets and asset-intensive reinsurance means the risk to global financial stability is currently relatively small, while flagging the rapid growth as something to monitor. That is a careful sentence and it should be quoted as written rather than compressed into either direction.

3. What the popular critique gets wrong

The strongest version of the concern is a governance and measurement argument. The version that circulates is often a regulatory one, and it is mistaken on three specific points that are easy to check.

Bermuda is not an unregulated jurisdiction. Its commercial insurance regime holds full Solvency II equivalence from the European Commission and NAIC Qualified and Reciprocal Jurisdiction status, which removes the collateral requirement that once applied. Roughly 80 per cent of Bermuda reinsurance business is conducted on a collateralised basis, largely through funds withheld and modified coinsurance, with the assets sitting on the ceding insurer’s own balance sheet. There is a genuine transparency argument about offshore structures. There is no supportable claim that the assets are unsupervised.

The Federal Reserve has not flagged this as systemic. Its Financial Stability Report of May 2026 contains no occurrence of the words “reinsurance” or “Bermuda” in seventy-one pages. What it discusses in relation to insurers is leverage, illiquid assets and nontraditional liabilities in general terms.

Regulators have not defined private equity ownership, deliberately. On the NAIC’s own list of regulatory considerations for private-equity-owned insurers, the item titled “Definition of Private Equity” is the one entry with no update in any reporting period. The FSOC states the position directly: supervisory focus is on investment behaviour rather than on who owns the company. That is the right call analytically, and it means “PE-owned” is not the variable a regulator is actually watching.

4. The mechanism, stated precisely

Strip out the rhetoric and the structure is straightforward, and it is disclosed. An asset manager acquires or partners with a life and annuity company. The insurer’s liabilities are long dated and do not redeem like fund capital, so they function as permanent capital. The manager earns fees on assets it would otherwise have to raise, and its origination business gains a reliable buyer. The insurer, in exchange, gains access to origination it could not build itself and, in principle, a yield advantage over public credit at the same rating.

The KKR transaction is the best-documented instance, because the filings are unusually clear. The 2021 purchase price was $4.74bn, of which KKR’s own cash was $2.91bn; the remainder came from co-investors and rolling shareholders. For that, KKR consolidated a balance sheet carrying $99.5bn of investments against $100.4bn of policy liabilities. In January 2024 it acquired the remaining 36.7 per cent for about $2.6bn. Both tranches were priced at one times book value.

Two measurement points follow, and they apply to every firm in the table.

“Assets under management” is not a consistent unit here. KKR’s definition includes the asset value of the insurance companies themselves. That is a balance sheet, not third-party managed money, and comparing it to a traditional manager’s AUM is a category error.

Headline growth includes capital that is not the insurer’s. Of Global Atlantic’s roughly $213bn of reported AUM at the end of 2025, $58bn is third-party capital in sponsored sidecar vehicles that did not exist at the baseline. On a like-for-like measure of invested assets the figure went from $144.3bn at the end of 2024 to $158.7bn a year later.

5. What would actually tell you something

The useful questions are about behaviour and disclosure, not ownership.

What share of the portfolio is affiliate-originated, and is it disclosed? The NAIC’s work on affiliated-investment reporting is the most consequential regulatory development in this area, precisely because it addresses the variable that matters.

Do the capital charges match the risk? Risk-based capital treatment for private credit, asset-backed finance and structured tranches rests on a ratings process designed for corporate bonds. If the charge understates the risk, the capital efficiency is the return. The NAIC’s work on CLO modelling is the live test of this.

Does liability optionality meet asset illiquidity? Annuities carry surrender features. An illiquidity premium earned against liabilities the holder can exercise is a different object from one earned against liabilities that cannot run.

And the correlation nobody sees on a single balance sheet. If a growing share of the industry holds similar private credit originated by a small number of managers, sector-level correlation is higher than any individual filing shows. That is the concern Working Paper No. 31 raised about the high-yield index, and it does not become smaller when the buyer is an insurer.

6. What this note does not claim

It does not claim that private-equity-owned insurers are less safe. The evidence for that would be comparative default, downgrade or impairment experience, and this note does not present it.

It does not claim wrongdoing by any firm. The structure described is disclosed in public filings and operates under state and, where applicable, Bermudian supervision.

It does not claim the phenomenon is systemic. The official bodies that would say so have declined to, and their language is quoted above rather than paraphrased.

What it claims is narrower and, on the evidence, solid: the phenomenon is real and quantified at 7.8 per cent of industry assets and $849 billion of life insurer private credit; the popular account of it is unreliable in most of its specifics; and the questions worth asking are about affiliated-asset disclosure, capital charges and liability optionality rather than about who owns the shares.

Method
Thirteen rows of a circulating table checked claim by claim against primary sources. 104 discrete claims; 46 confirmed, 27 contradicted, 26 partly confirmed, 5 unverifiable. Every correction in section 2 traces to a filing.
Sources
SEC filings for KKR, Apollo, Blackstone, Brookfield, TPG and Jackson Financial; statutory annual statements; NAIC Capital Markets Bureau special report on private-equity-owned insurers; FSOC annual report; IAIS Global Insurance Market Report; Bermuda Monetary Authority; Federal Reserve Financial Stability Report, May 2026; Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago research on life insurer private credit.
Honesty
One row of the original table is omitted rather than corrected, because it concerned an unadjudicated federal matter and no charges have been filed against anyone named. This note takes no position on it.
