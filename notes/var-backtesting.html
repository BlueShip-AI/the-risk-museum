The Light Was Green

by V. Dimopoulos · Research note #10 · July 25, 2026 · three standard VaR models, twenty years of the S&P 500, one honest backtest · July 25, 2026

Value-at-Risk is a promise with a number attached: on 99 days out of 100, tomorrow’s loss will not exceed this. The museum’s whole premise is that a number is worth exactly what its backtest says. So before you trust a VaR, you backtest it. This note backtests three of the standard ones on SPY, the S&P 500 ETF, using twenty years of daily returns and grading them strictly out of sample: every VaR quoted for a day is built only from returns before that day, then checked against a return it never got to see.

The test

Three ways to put a number on the 1% and 5% tail of tomorrow, each re-estimated every day on a rolling 500-day window (about two years): Historical Simulation reads the empirical quantile straight off the window; the Gaussian assumes a normal and uses the window’s mean and standard deviation; EWMA is the RiskMetrics recipe, a zero-mean normal whose variance decays at λ = 0.94, so it forgets old calm quickly. All three are graded on the identical evaluation sample, 4,922 one-day forecasts from December 2006 to July 2026, so the comparison is like for like. An exception is a day whose realized loss breached the VaR. The coverage tests are Kupiec’s (is the exception rate right?) and Christoffersen’s conditional-coverage test (is the rate right and are the exceptions independent, or do they cluster?). Kupiec and Christoffersen p-values below 0.05 reject the model. A p of 0.0000 is the code’s four-decimal print of a value too small to show. It is not an exact zero. The Basel traffic light is defined only for the 99% test.

What twenty years says

All three models fail Kupiec’s unconditional-coverage test at both 99% and 95%: every one of them under-covers the tail. The Gaussian is worst. It budgeted for 49 breaches at 99% and took 145, a 2.95% exception rate on a number that promised 1%, roughly three times the losses it said it was insuring against. Historical Simulation is the least-bad on the full sample at 1.75%, EWMA sits between at 2.42%. Christoffersen’s conditional-coverage test is rejected for all three, which means the breaches are not scattered evenly. They arrive in clusters. That is the tell. A VaR that is wrong in bursts is worse than one that is wrong at random, because the bursts are the days you built it for.

Now the ranking flips. In 2008 the Gaussian took 28 breaches where 2.5 were expected and Historical Simulation took 20, both because a trailing window is slow to admit that the world has changed, and a 99% number built on last year’s calm is a fiction once the calm breaks. EWMA, whose variance reacts within days, took 6. COVID’s near-vertical crash is the harder case: even EWMA took 6 against 0.6 expected, because no one-day model front-runs an overnight gap. But the window methods took 10 and 12. The model with the best full-sample rate, Historical Simulation, is not the model you want in a storm.

The green light

Here is the part a risk committee should sit with. Basel’s traffic light looks only at the most recent 250 days. Today that window is quiet, so all three models glow green, a clean bill of health from the one test a regulator reads first. The same three models, over twenty years, under-cover their tails; the 2008 column says two of them come apart exactly when it matters. A green light describes the last twelve months. It says nothing about the model. The backtest that spans the crises is the one that tells you what you actually own.

The verdict

No 1-day VaR here covered its tail. All three failed Kupiec at 99% and 95%, and the Gaussian failed worst. EWMA held up best through the crises, and it is not the model today’s traffic light rewards. None of these numbers would earn a promotion in this museum; they earn a plaque for failing honestly, which is the only kind of failure worth hanging.

Declared limits

Three, stated plainly. One instrument. This is SPY alone. It is a single, deeply liquid index with continuous history, the easy case for VaR. A book of individual names, some of which delisted, is strictly harder and its VaR would look worse; the museum’s wider price panel is survivorship-biased, and this study sidesteps that only by testing the index itself. The normal tail. Both parametric models assume a bell curve, which understates fat tails even when the volatility estimate is right. That is most of why the Gaussian and EWMA under-cover, and it is a modelling choice rather than an accident of this sample. The window is a knob. Five hundred days is a choice; a shorter window reacts faster to a regime change but estimates the 1% quantile from fewer tail points, and the honest reading is that no single window wins in both calm and crisis.

This hangs in the museum because most VaR you meet in the wild is quoted but never backtested, a number on a risk report with no scorecard behind it. The museum’s job is to run the scorecard and publish it even when the answer is “all three failed, and here is how.” A number that fails a test you can see is more useful than a number that passes a test nobody ran.
